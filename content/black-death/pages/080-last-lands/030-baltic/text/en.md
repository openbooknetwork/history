[Lübeck](@lubeck), often called the leading town of the Hanseatic merchants, and [Hamburg](@hamburg-1350) have the plague by early summer 1350, and in [Bremen](@bremen-1350) it is at its worst in June or July. Bremen’s oldest book of citizens records 6,966 known dead in the town’s four parishes, besides the countless common people who died in the streets.

Denmark has no eyewitness account of the plague. Historians trace it through gifts, wills and the odd list of the dead, and by one account the gifts for masses for the dead rise sharply in 1350. By that reckoning the plague is at [Ribe](@ribe-1350) from July to October 1350, at [Copenhagen](@copenhagen-1350) in August and September, and at Roskilde in September and October.

Sweden and Norway share a king, Magnus Eriksson. While the plague is killing his Norwegian subjects, probably in September 1349, he writes from [Lödöse](@lodose-1349) that “most of the people who lived in the lands West of our land are now dead”. To soften God’s anger, every Swede is to fast on bread and water each Friday, hear mass, confess and give to the poor. [Visby](@visby-1350) on Gotland has the plague by July 1350 at the latest, probably from a ship out of Denmark or Germany. Nine people there confess under torture to causing it with poison, and in July they are burned. From Gotland it appears to cross to central Sweden, and it reaches [Stockholm](@stockholm-1350) in August. In the autumn it probably comes overland from Norway into the west of the kingdom.

Further east lies Prussia, the state of the Teutonic Order. The plague breaks out at [Elbląg](@elblag-1349) (then Elbing) on 24 August 1349, probably brought by a ship back from Oslo, almost a year before it reaches Lübeck. From there it goes on into Poland.
