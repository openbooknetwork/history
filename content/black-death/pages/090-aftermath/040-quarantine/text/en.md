Ragusa (today Dubrovnik) is a small merchant republic on the Adriatic, under the protection of the king of Hungary since 1358. Its ships trade with the Balkans, Italy and the Levant, and the plague keeps coming back with them. On 27 July 1377 its Great Council decrees that nobody coming from a place struck by plague may enter the city or its territory until they have spent a month apart, on the islet of [Mrkan](@mrkan-1377) or in the town of Cavtat. Anyone from the city who visits them there must stay the thirty days too. The month gives a hidden infection time to show. The decree is kept in the Council’s book of decisions, the Liber viridis, which survives in the State Archives in Dubrovnik.

Keeping the sick apart is older. The historian of medicine Mirko Grmek showed that Ragusa was the first state to hold arrivals apart for a fixed time before letting them in. Its waiting time of thirty days is called the trentino. Italian ports adopt similar laws within a century. [Marseille](@marseille-1383) takes up the quarantine in 1383, and in 1476 turns a leper house into a plague hospital.

Venice goes further. On the island of Santa Maria di Nazareth in the lagoon it keeps a hospital for the plague sick from 1403, and in 1423 it opens there the first permanent lazaretto, where crews and goods wait before they enter the city. The island is later called the [Lazzaretto Vecchio](@lazzaretto-vecchio), the old lazaretto, after Venice opens a second one in 1468. The waiting time grows to forty days, the quarantena in the Venetian language, which gives the word quarantine. Digs on the island since 2004 have brought up the bones of more than 1,500 plague dead. Ragusa has a lazaretto of its own at Danče, west of the city, from the 1460s, and builds a larger one outside its eastern gate between 1590 and 1642.
