Early in 323 BC Alexander comes down from [Ecbatana](@ecbatana-323) to [Babylon](@babylon-323). Nearchus's fleet has come up the Euphrates from the Gulf. Forty-seven warships from Phoenicia have come overland in sections to [Thapsacus](@thapsacus-323), been rebuilt there and floated down the Euphrates. The first target is [Arabia](@arabia-323); the pretext, Arrian says, is that its people alone in the region have sent the king no embassy. For Bosworth this was the first step of far larger western plans. The routes on the map are approximate.

After a night of drinking with his Companion Medius of Larissa, Alexander falls ill with a fever at the end of May 323 BC, on 18 Daesius in the Royal Journals, a daily court record. For some days he still sacrifices and plans the voyage. Then the fever grows, he loses his voice, and his soldiers file past his bed. A Babylonian astronomical diary dates his death to 29 Aiaru, by modern reckoning from the evening of 10 June to that of 11 June 323 BC. He was 32 years and 8 months old, by Aristobulus's account.

What killed him is not known. The Journals describe only a fever. Arrian and Plutarch report a rumour that Antipater, the regent of Macedon, had him poisoned, and in Arrian's version Antipater's son Cassander brought the poison. Plutarch writes that no one suspected poison at first.

After the death Perdiccas finds the king's memoranda, which Diodorus lists: a thousand warships against Carthage and the west, a road along Libya's coast to the Pillars of Heracles, six great temples, and a tomb for Philip as large as Egypt's greatest pyramid. Read to the army, they are all set aside. In Plutarch's account Roxana, Alexander's pregnant wife, lures Stateira with a forged letter and, with Perdiccas's knowledge, has her and her sister killed.
