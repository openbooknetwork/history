[The bastille of Saint-Loup](@saint-loup) stands on the north bank east of Orléans, above the landing-place on the road to Chécy: about a mile and a half from the city by Lowell's reckoning, a league by Lang's.

On Wednesday 4 May, the eve of the Ascension, a few hours after the army from Blois has come in, the French go out against it at about noon, about 1,500 men by the Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of the city. The Journal has Joan and the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois) lead them out together. In the Chronique de la Pucelle and in Ramsay, and in the memories of her confessor Jean Pasquerel and her page Louis de Coutes, she is resting at her lodging when it begins. She wakes, arms and rides to the fort with her standard, and with her coming the attack grows stronger.

While its garrison defends it hard, English come out from the other bastilles to help.\* The city's belfry rings twice in warning, the French draw up in the fields between, and the English turn back to their forts. Towards vespers Saint-Loup is taken by force, burned and pulled down. The assault has lasted three hours by the Journal, and more than four or five by a letter Charles VII wrote on 10 May.\*\* Some English who hid in the church tower in priests' vestments are spared at Joan's word and brought into the city, says the Chronique.

Saint-Loup is the first English fort to fall. At her trial in 1431 Joan said she went first to Saint-Loup and then to the fort at the bridge. Its fall cleared the approaches east of the city, in Lowell's judgement.

### Notes

\* The Journal has them come from the bastille of Paris at Saint-Pouair. The Chronique and the chronicle of the feast of 8 May have Talbot lead them out of the other bastilles, as far as the pavé de Fleury in the chronicle. Where Talbot himself was is told differently: Champion and Ramsay put him in Saint-Loup, and the Chronique says he had garrisoned it well; Lang puts his main force at Saint-Laurent, and Anatole France has him set out from there.

\*\* The Journal counts 114 English killed and 40 taken. The Chronique puts the dead at 160, and Guillaume Girault, a notary of Orléans, at more than 120 taken and dead. The chronicle of the feast has 120 to 140 English in the fort, and Monstrelet a garrison of 300 to 400. Charles VII's letter says all the English inside were killed, and only two of the French.
