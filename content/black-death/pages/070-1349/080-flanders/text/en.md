Gilles Li Muisis, abbot of St Martin’s at [Tournai](@tournai), is about 77 when the plague reaches his town. His chronicle runs to 1349. By his account the plague is at Tournai from August 1349, in the weeks when the flagellants arrive, until November. The miniatures that Pierart dou Tielt paints for his chronicle show the townspeople carrying coffins to new graves.

Taking the plague for a punishment sent by God, the magistrates of Tournai set out to stamp out sin: gambling is forbidden, and men and women who share a house unmarried must wed at once or separate. Their register of bans was lost when the city was bombarded in May 1940. Its first 30 pages survive in copies by the town archivist Frédéric Hennebert, who died in 1857, and the historians Claire Billen and Marc Boone published them in 2021. The town’s leaders, it is said, stay in the city through the epidemic.

The plague is in Hainaut and Flanders by the summer of 1349, and its path further north cannot be followed. The death duties of the county of Hainaut show that it killed many there, women more than men. In Holland the draining of the wetlands stops at this time, perhaps because with fewer people there is land enough for all.

In the autumn the plague reaches the French court. Bonne of Luxembourg, the wife of John, Duke of Normandy and heir to the throne, dies of it at [Maubuisson](@bonne-of-luxembourg) near Pontoise on 11 September 1349, aged 34. [Queen Joan of Burgundy](@joan-of-burgundy), the wife of Philip VI, dies of the plague on 12 December 1349, in Paris by one account.
