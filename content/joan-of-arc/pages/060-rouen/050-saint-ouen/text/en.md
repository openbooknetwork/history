On the morning of Thursday 24 May 1431 Joan is taken from the castle to [the cemetery of Saint-Ouen](@saint-ouen-1431) in Rouen, the open ground that Lowell places just south of the abbey church. On a scaffold before her judges and a great crowd she hears a sermon by Guillaume Erard. Cardinal Beaufort, great-uncle of Henry VI, sits among them, with bishops of Thérouanne, Noyon and Norwich. Then the sentence is read, and before it reaches its end she abjures. Jean Massieu, the usher of the court, said that he handed her a pen and that she made a cross with it.\*

She is sentenced to prison for life, "with the bread of sorrow and water of affliction". Some of those present, by Massieu's account, ask that she be taken to a church prison, but Pierre Cauchon, the Bishop of Beauvais who leads the court, sends her back to [the castle of Rouen](@castle-relapse-1431). That afternoon she puts on a woman's dress.

On Monday 28 May, the day after Trinity Sunday, the judges find her in her cell in man's dress again. The record has her say that her voices told her she had damned herself to save her life, and that she had recanted only for fear of the fire.\*\* Two friars of the court, Martin Ladvenu and Isambart de La Pierre, testified in 1450 that she blamed her prison. Ladvenu said she told him she had been beaten and molested there and insulted by an English lord, and that this was why she wore the man's dress again; Isambart heard her say before many that the English had done her "great wrong and violence" while she wore the woman's dress.

On Tuesday 29 May the judges and assessors meet in the chapel of [the archbishop's palace](@archbishops-palace-1431) in Rouen. They find her relapsed and summon her to [the Old Market](@old-market-summons-1431) for eight the next morning. Thurston counts thirty-seven judges.

On the map her road there and back only joins the two places, since no source gives the streets.

### Notes

\* What she abjured is not certain. The record gives a long formula of abjuration. Witnesses of 1450 to 1456 said the paper read to her was short: about six lines of large writing, in the words of the notary Nicolas Taquel, and a few lines by the account of the five witnesses, Massieu among them, whom Thurston cites. Thurston holds that its terms will never be known.

\*\* Why she put the man's dress on again is told three ways. The record has her say that she took it of her own will, as more fitting among men, and because what she was promised, to hear Mass and to have her chains taken off, was not kept. Massieu had it from her that on Trinity Sunday the English guards stripped away her woman's clothes and left her nothing to wear but the man's. Thurston reports the charge, made later, that her jailers set a trap for her and that Cauchon was party to it.
