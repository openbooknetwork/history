Once Rouen is his, Charles VII has the trial that condemned Joan in 1431 looked into. In February 1450 he commissions Guillaume Bouillé, a doctor of theology, who hears seven witnesses at [Rouen](@rouen-1456) on 4 and 5 March, and the matter goes no further. In late April and May 1452 Cardinal Guillaume d'Estouteville, papal legate in France, opens a second enquiry at Rouen with Jean Bréhal, Inquisitor of France; some twenty witnesses are heard.

On 11 June 1455 Pope Calixtus III authorises a review of the case. His judges are Jean Juvénal des Ursins, Archbishop of Reims, Guillaume Chartier, Bishop of Paris, and Richard Olivier de Longueil, Bishop of Coutances, and they take Bréhal with them. The court holds its first sitting in [Notre-Dame](@notre-dame-1455) in Paris on 7 November 1455. Joan's mother, Isabelle Romée, in mourning, comes before the judges with her sons Jean d'Arc and Pierre d'Arc, shows them the pope's letter and, weeping, presents her petition. Men of Orléans, where she has lived for fifteen years, come with her. A second public sitting follows on 17 November.

@image isabelle-romee-retrial-miniature-2b4164

The witnesses are questioned where they live: in January and February 1456 at [Domrémy](@domremy-1456) and Vaucouleurs, in February and March at [Orléans](@orleans-1456), in April and May at Paris, and at Rouen in December and again in May. On 28 May her squire, Jean d'Aulon, gives his evidence at [Lyon](@lyon-1456).

The judges give their verdict at Rouen, in the great hall of the archbishop's palace, at eight o'clock on Wednesday 7 July 1456; the Archbishop of Reims reads it out. The trial of 1431 and its sentences, with the abjuration and the execution, are declared "null, non-existent, without value or effect". The sentence is proclaimed that day at Saint-Ouen and the next in the Old Market, where a cross is put up.
