The plague reaches the Russian lands last, from the Baltic in the west. It comes from the area of today’s Latvia to [Pskov](@pskov), by one account a merchant town on the road between [Novgorod](@novgorod) and the German towns of Livonia. It arrives just before the winter of 1351, which holds it back until spring 1352. The sick of Pskov spit blood and die on the second or third day, and barely a third of the townspeople survive. Many take monastic vows and wait for death in a monastery, and others give their property to the church or the poor.

Called to Pskov, [Vasily Kalika](@vasily-kalika), the archbishop of Novgorod and Pskov, leads a procession round the town. On the way home he falls ill, it seems of the plague, and on 3 July 1352 he dies at the monastery of St Michael at the mouth of the Uza, on the Shelon river. The plague reaches Novgorod in August 1352 by a path no source gives. There, the town’s chronicle says, “a man would spit blood and after three days he was dead”. Hundreds die every day in both towns, it is said.

The Novgorod chronicler believes that the plague “passed over the face of all the land”. One account has it spread south along the trade routes. In the north it reaches Beloozero, where no one is left alive, and the town is later rebuilt on a new site, where [Belozersk](@belozersk-1352) stands today.

In 1353 the plague reaches [Moscow](@moscow-1353), though the chronicles do not name the town. Theognostus, the metropolitan of Kyiv and all Rus’, who has his seat there, dies on 11 March after a long illness. In the same week, the chronicle says, the two young sons of Grand Prince [Simeon](@simeon), called the Proud, die. Simeon takes monastic vows and dies on 27 April, aged 35, and his brother Andrei six weeks later. The chronicle names no illness, and historians take these deaths for the plague. Simeon’s will, written on paper, survives. His brother Ivan II succeeds him.
