In 1839 President Martin Van Buren sends the American writer John Lloyd Stephens to Central America as special envoy. Stephens sails to Belize with the English architect and draughtsman Frederick Catherwood. Both have read Juan Galindo’s report on the ruins of Copán. They go there first, spend two weeks mapping the site, and Stephens buys the ruins for 50 dollars. While they travel, the Federal Republic of Central America falls apart in civil war.

In 1840 Catherwood draws two stelae at Quiriguá. The two men cross the Guatemalan highlands by way of Iximche, Q'umarkaj and Zaculeu, visit Toniná and reach Palenque on 11 May 1840. There they draw the Temple of the Inscriptions, the Temple of the Cross and the Temple of the Sun. They go on to Uxmal. Stephens’s Incidents of Travel in Central America, Chiapas, and Yucatan, with engravings after Catherwood’s drawings, appears in 1841 and sells widely.

In October 1841 they return to Yucatán. Over the next months they record Mayapan, Uxmal, Kabah, Sayil, Labna, Chichen Itza and Tulum, several of them for the first time, for Incidents of Travel in Yucatan (1843). In 1844 Catherwood publishes 25 colour lithographs as Views of Ancient Monuments in Central America, Chiapas and Yucatan. The books argue that the ancestors of the Maya living in the region built the ruined cities, against theories that credited peoples from Europe or Asia.
