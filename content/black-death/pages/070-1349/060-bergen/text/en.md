The Icelandic annals tell how the plague comes to Norway. Two Icelandic bishops, Orm of Hólar and Gyrd Ivarsson of Skálholt, are in Norway when it arrives, and the annals draw on what they saw. In August 1349 a ship from England reaches [Bergen](@bergen). The sailors begin to die once the cargo is ashore, the townspeople soon after, and from Bergen the plague travels on over the rest of Norway. The annals name no English port.

[Oslo](@oslo) has the plague earlier, by May or June 1349, though no one left an account of it there. Already in February 1349 an altar to St Sebastian, the protector against plague, is founded in Oslo cathedral.

In the autumn the plague reaches [Trondheim](@trondheim), the seat of the archbishop of Nidaros. Archbishop Arne Einarsson dies with all but one of the canons of his cathedral. Every bishop of Norway except the bishop of Oslo dies. In 1371 the archdiocese of Nidaros has 40 priests, against 300 before the plague. The last known victim, Bishop Guttorm Pålsson of Stavanger, dies on 7 January 1350.

The annals say that a third of the people died. Modern estimates run to 60 or 65 per cent. Of about 36,500 farms in Norway around 1300, about 16,000 are still worked in 1520, and the population does not return to its level of 1300 until the 17th century. In the folk tales the plague lives on as an old woman, Pesta, who goes from farm to farm with a rake and a broom. Where she rakes, some live. Where she sweeps, no one does.
