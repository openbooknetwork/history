The plague leaves fewer hands to plough, reap and weave, and the survivors ask for more pay. Lords and employers turn to their kings. On 18 June 1349, while the plague is still spreading through England, Edward III issues the Ordinance of Labourers at [Westminster](@westminster-1349). Every man and woman under sixty who has no land or trade of their own must work for whoever asks, at the wages of 1346–47 or the years before. Nobody may give alms to beggars who can work. In 1351 Parliament turns the ordinance into the Statute of Labourers and sets the rates: a reaper takes 2 pence a day in the first week of August, a master mason 4 pence. The justices who enforce it in the 1350s charge workers with taking too much pay far more often than with any other offence, the historian Bertha Putnam finds.

Other rulers do the same. John II of France issues an ordinance on the trades of Paris on 30 January 1351, and that autumn the Cortes of Castile at [Valladolid](@valladolid-1351) fix the pay of workers. In the long run wages rise all the same, because employers short of hands pay more. The economic historian Gregory Clark finds that the average English farm wage doubled from 1350 to 1450.

Revolts follow in the next decades. Historians weigh the plague’s part in each against war, taxes and local quarrels. The Jacquerie, a rising of peasants north of Paris, begins at [Saint-Leu-d’Esserent](@saint-leu-1358) on 28 May 1358, while John II is a prisoner of the English. In 1378 the wool workers of [Florence](@ciompi-1378), the Ciompi, rise and hold a share of the city’s government from July to the end of August. In England a poll tax sets off the Peasants’ Revolt in Essex on 30 May 1381. The rebels enter London on 13 June and kill Simon Sudbury, archbishop of Canterbury, the next day. At Mile End Richard II grants charters that free the serfs. At Smithfield on 15 June the king’s party kills the rebels’ leader, Wat Tyler, and on 2 July the charters are revoked.
