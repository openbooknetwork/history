The doctors of 1348 explain the plague with the learning of their time: Greek medicine as Arabic writers enlarged it, and astrology. Most blame air corrupted by the stars, by rotting matter or by vapours from the earth.

In Paris, at the request of King Philip VI, the masters of the [faculty of medicine](@paris-faculty) write a report, which they deliver in October 1348. Its distant cause, they write, is a conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in Aquarius on 20 March 1345, which corrupted the air. Advice under the faculty’s name calls for fires in houses and squares and a calm mind, and warns against bathing.

[Gentile da Foligno](@gentile-da-foligno), a famous teacher of medicine at Perugia, writes several opinions on the plague in 1348. He holds that it can pass to anyone near the sick, carried, some say, on their breath. He advises bloodletting at the first signs and, by some accounts, theriac, an old remedy against poisons. By a pupil’s note, he falls ill on 22 June from tending the sick and dies at Foligno six days later.

In al-Andalus two writers give more weight to what they see. [Ibn Khatima](@ibn-khatima), a doctor and poet of Almería, watches the plague in his city from June 1348, it is said, and finishes a treatise on it in 1349. He blames corrupted air, but he also holds that the sick should be kept apart. One account has him notice that those who come near them fall ill, while those who keep away mostly stay well. [Ibn al-Khatib](@ibn-al-khatib), the secretary and later vizier of the sultans of Granada, writes that experience and reliable reports prove contagion: clothes and dishes carry it, a healthy port falls ill when a ship comes in from a stricken land, and people who live apart stay well. By one account he adds that a saying of the Prophet must be read in the light of such proof. The religious teaching of the time holds that plague comes from God and not from contagion, and that a Muslim should neither flee a stricken land nor enter it.
