At [Gien](@gien-rendezvous), where Joan and the Duke of Alençon join Charles VII from [Orléans](@orleans-24-june-1429) on 24 June, the princes and captains want the king to go into Normandy, and others want the upper Loire cleared first. Joan holds out for Reims, and the council gives way. On Wednesday 29 June the king leaves Gien. He has no money to pay the army, and knights, squires and common men follow Joan unpaid. Friar Richard, a preacher lately driven out of Paris, goes with the army.

The road runs through towns most of which have held for the English and the Burgundians since about the Treaty of Troyes in 1420. [Auxerre](@auxerre-truce), a Burgundian town, keeps its gates shut but sells the army food under a truce, and promises to do as Troyes, Châlons and Reims do. After about three days the king moves on. The citizens are said to have paid Georges de La Trémoille, the king's favourite, 2,000 écus in secret to be spared an assault. [Saint-Florentin](@saint-florentin-1429) submits on the road beyond.

[Troyes](@troyes-1429) refuses to open, and its garrison, 500 to 600 English and Burgundians, sallies out. The king is before the town on Friday 8 July. The army is short of food. At a council the captains hear that it lacks artillery and money, and most think the town too strong. Robert le Maçon, of the king's council, asks that Joan be heard. She promises Troyes within two or three days, by love or by force, and makes ready an assault. The bishop and the townsmen come out to treat: the garrison may leave with its goods, and the town is pardoned. On Sunday 10 July Charles VII enters Troyes.

Auxerre stays Burgundian.
