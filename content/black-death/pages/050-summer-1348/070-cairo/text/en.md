The plague reaches [Cairo](@cairo) from [Alexandria](@alexandria) by September 1348. Cairo, the seat of the Mamluk sultans, has more people than any city of Europe or anywhere else around the Mediterranean, and the plague there is at its worst from October 1348 to January 1349. About 200,000 people die by one estimate, a third of the city.

Al-Maqrizi, a historian of Cairo in the next century, reports about 7,000 deaths a day at the peak, around December 1348. One account has him work from the records of the time, which count the dead each day as the numbers rise through the autumn. By the same account the men who wash the dead, dig the graves and recite the Quran at funerals can ask any price, and many who take the property of the dead do not live to enjoy it. Ibn Battuta, the traveller, was told in Cairo that 21,000 people had died there in a single day.

The sultan, al-Nasir Hasan, is a boy of about thirteen. On 25 September he leaves the city with his court and stays at Siryaqus, north of Cairo, until 22 December. In the city the people hold mass prayers in the mosques and at the cemeteries.

Early in 1349 the plague moves up the Nile into Upper Egypt. Its villagers die, or flee to the towns and refuse to come back to the landlords’ estates, and fields go unsown. The district of [Asyut](@asyut) has 6,000 taxpayers before the plague and 116 after it. Egypt’s farmland depends on canals and dikes that need many hands. Without those hands they fall into ruin, and Egypt recovers from the plague more slowly than England.

In 1356 Sultan Hasan begins the great mosque and college below the citadel of Cairo that bears his name, at a cost of more than a million dinars. Much of the money, it is said, comes from the property of plague dead who left no heirs.
