After Joan and the French relieve [Orléans](@orleans-8-may) in May 1429, its bishop, the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois) and the townspeople decide that each year on 8 May a procession shall walk with candles to the Augustins, stopping to pray wherever there has been fighting. The city accounts pay for it in 1435, and that year two men are given money toward the stages on which they play a "certain mistère" at the bridge during the procession.

A fortnight after the sentence of 1456 the court orders a procession at Orléans too, with two of the judges in it. The procession of 8 May is kept almost without a break for more than 450 years.

Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini, later Pope Pius II, writes of her as a girl inspired by the divine spirit, and Archbishop Antoninus of Florence holds her to be led by the spirit of God. In England she is thought a witch as late as Shakespeare's time, yet by the nineteenth century Southey, Hallam, Carlyle and De Quincey are among the English writers who pay her honour.

On 8 May 1869, after preaching in her praise at Orléans, Bishop Félix Dupanloup has the bishops present sign a letter asking the Holy See to make her a saint. The case opens at Orléans in 1874. Leo XIII signs the decree that introduces her cause on 27 January 1894, and she may now be called venerable. On 6 January 1904 Pius X declares that her virtues were heroic. The brief of her beatification is published on 11 April 1909, and she is beatified in St Peter's at Rome on 18 April.
