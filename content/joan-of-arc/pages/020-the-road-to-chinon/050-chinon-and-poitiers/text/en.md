Joan reaches [Chinon](@chinon-audience) in late February or early March 1429 and lodges at an inn. She goes up to the castle after dinner, or after Charles VII has made her wait two days. Louis de Bourbon, Count of Vendôme, brings her in. She picks out the king among his courtiers, by the counsel of her voice, and tells him she has come to make war on the English. Until he is anointed at Reims she calls him the Dauphin.

The king has a sign before he believes her, and she calls him the true heir of France. What the sign is is not known.

At [Poitiers](@poitiers-examination) churchmen and doctors, with Regnault de Chartres, Archbishop of Reims and Chancellor of France, examine her; no record survives. They find nothing in her against the faith, and judge that in the kingdom's need the king may use her. Yolande of Aragon, the king's mother-in-law, questions her too. Her letter bidding the English before Orléans go home is dated at Poitiers on 22 March 1429.

From about mid-April, at [Tours](@tours-armour), she is given armour and a household: a squire, Jean d'Aulon, two pages, and Jean Pasquerel, an Augustinian friar, as confessor. A painter of Tours is paid 25 livres tournois for her banners. At her word an armourer of Tours fetches a rusty sword marked with five crosses from behind the altar at [Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois](@fierbois-sword). Her voices, she holds, have told her of it.

After about ten days at Tours she goes up the Loire to [Blois](@blois-gathering) with the Archbishop of Reims and Raoul de Gaucourt, the Grand Steward. There the convoy for Orléans gathers under the Gascon captain La Hire and the marshals Gilles de Rais and Jean de Brosse, lord of Boussac.
