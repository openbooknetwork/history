Early on Sunday 8 May the English leave their bastilles north of the river, setting fire to some of them, and draw up for battle before the city in [one array](@english-array). The French come out and draw up facing them. Joan, in a light coat of mail because of her wound, forbids them to begin a fight on a Sunday: if the English attack, they are to defend themselves boldly, but they are not to attack. In the Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of the city, the two armies stand face to face for a whole hour; Monstrelet, a Burgundian chronicler, says only that the English waited a long time. The Chronique de la Pucelle has priests sing hymns in the field and two masses said before the army on a portable altar.

Then the English march away in good order. Talbot and Lord Scales go to [Meung](@meung-may-1429) and [Beaugency](@beaugency-may-1429), and Suffolk to [Jargeau](@jargeau-may-1429), by the Chronique. Champion has Talbot go to Meung first and then to Beaugency, and Ramsay has some of the English go to each town. The townspeople plunder the empty bastilles and pull them down, and their guns are hauled into the city.

Joan and the captains ride back in to give thanks, and Orléans holds a procession with a sermon, as Pasquerel, her confessor, remembered. The siege has lasted about seven months, from 12 October 1428, the Journal's first day.\* The English still hold the Loire bridges of Meung, Beaugency and Jargeau, above and below the city. It is decided to go and seek the king, and Joan goes with the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois) and the captains.

### Notes

\* Monstrelet counts 6,000 to 8,000 English killed or taken in the three days of fighting, and fewer than 100 French dead. Charles VII's letter of 10 May gives only two French dead at Saint-Loup. Joan told her judges in 1431 that there were a hundred wounded or more.
