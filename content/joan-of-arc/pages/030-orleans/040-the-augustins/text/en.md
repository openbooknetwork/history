No one fights on Thursday 5 May, the feast of the Ascension.\* A council decides to attack the Tourelles and the English works on the south bank, so that the city can again be fed from Berry. Her confessor Jean Pasquerel says she sent the English a last summons tied to an arrow.

On Friday 6 May about 4,000 men, the figure of the Journal of the Siege, cross the Loire in boats from near the Tour Neuve, the tower at the east end of the walls. They come to an island, which Ramsay calls [the Île Saint-Aignan](@ile-saint-aignan); its site is lost. From it, as Joan's squire Jean d'Aulon tells it, they cross the narrow southern arm on a bridge of two boats.\*\* William Glasdale, captain of the Tourelles, empties and burns [the bastille of Saint-Jean-le-Blanc](@saint-jean-le-blanc) and draws his men back to [the Augustins](@augustins), a fort in a ruined convent near the bridge.

Before the Augustins the French fall back towards the island, on a cry that the English of Saint-Privé are coming, says the Chronique de la Pucelle, or by the captains' decision, in d'Aulon's account. The English come out after them. Joan and La Hire turn with lances couched and drive them back, and before night the French take the Augustins by storm. The Chronique says she had it burned so that the men would not stop to plunder, and that she was hurt in the foot by a caltrop, a spiked iron.

That night the French lie before the Tourelles, and the townspeople bring them bread and wine. Joan is taken back into the city to sleep, by the Chronique and her page Louis de Coutes.\*\*\* In the dark English take to the river at [Saint-Privé](@saint-prive), and by morning its fort is empty.\*\*\*\*

### Notes

\* The Chronique de la Pucelle, which Ramsay follows, has Joan want to attack the bastille of Saint-Laurent and the captains refuse out of respect for the feast. Pasquerel and the Orléans chronicle of the feast of 8 May say it was she who would not fight. Lowell notes that the accounts do not agree.

\*\* The Journal calls the island "a small island facing Saint-Aignan", and the chronicle of the feast "an island before Saint-Jean-le-Blanc". The Journal has the French take Saint-Jean-le-Blanc at the first onset and only then fall back to the island.

\*\*\* D'Aulon has her stay before the fort all night with the lords and their men.

\*\*\*\* The Chronique and Ramsay have its garrison set fire to the fort and cross to Saint-Laurent. The chronicle of the feast has English try to cross there in two or three boats, and many drown; Lowell reads this as Talbot sending men to the south bank.
