In the winter of 1348–1349 the confessions taken at Chillon reach the towns of the Upper Rhine, and one town after another turns on its Jews before the plague arrives. At [Basel](@burning-basel) the guildsmen march on the town hall and force the council to give way. The Jews are shut into a wooden house on an island in the Rhine and burned on 16 January 1349. At [Speyer](@killing-speyer) the Jews are killed on 22 January, and their bodies are packed into wine casks and thrown into the Rhine. At [Freiburg](@burning-freiburg) the Jews are burned on 30 January.

At [Strasbourg](@burning-strasbourg) the council and its head, the Ammeister Peter Schwarber, protect the Jews. On 9 February the craftsmen gather in the cathedral square and force the masters out of office. Schwarber is banished, it is said, and the new council gives in to the crowd. On 14 February the Jews are burned in their cemetery. The chronicler Jacob Twinger von Königshofen, who follows Fritsche Closener’s account, counts about 2,000 dead. Those who accept baptism are spared, and many small children are baptised by force. Debts owed to the Jews are cancelled, and the council shares out their cash among the craftsmen. Königshofen writes: “The money was indeed the thing that killed the Jews.” The plague reaches Strasbourg on 8 July.

At [Worms](@burning-worms) on 1 March the Jewish quarter burns. Rather than be baptised by force, many Jews set fire to their houses, and chroniclers count more than 400 dead. At [Erfurt](@burning-erfurt) the community is destroyed on 21 and 22 March. Counts of the dead run from 100 to 3,000, and the plague comes to Erfurt only in 1350. In 1998 archaeologists find a hoard under a cellar wall there, which most likely belonged to the Jewish moneychanger Kalman of Wiehe: 24 kg of silver coins and ingots, and more than 700 pieces of goldsmiths’ work. Among them is a gold wedding ring engraved with the Hebrew words mazal tov, “good luck”.
