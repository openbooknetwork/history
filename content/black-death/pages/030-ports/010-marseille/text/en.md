In November 1347 ships from the east bring the plague to [Marseille](@marseille), the chief port of Provence, a county ruled by Queen Joanna I of Naples. The merchants of Marseille trade with Genoa, Sicily and the Levant, and the sickness comes in with their galleys.

From the harbour the plague moves inland, to Aix-en-Provence and up the Rhône to Arles. By January 1348 it is in [Avignon](@avignon), about 100 km from the sea. On the roads it needs about two months for that distance, less than 2 km a day. Ships have carried it from Sicily to Provence within weeks.

Avignon has been the seat of the popes since 1309. Its streets are crowded with clergy, envoys, merchants and pilgrims, and the sickness spreads fast among them. Louis Heyligen, a Flemish musician in the household of Cardinal Giovanni Colonna, writes from Avignon on 27 April 1348 that half the people have died. The pope has bought a field for a new cemetery, and Heyligen counts 11,000 bodies buried there in about six weeks.

In the same months ships bring the plague to the islands of [Corsica](@corsica) and [Sardinia](@sardinia). Corsica belongs to Genoa and most of Sardinia to the Crown of Aragon, and both lie on the sea lanes of the western Mediterranean. Both islands have the sickness early in 1348.
