After the fall of [Tyre](@tyre-332b) in summer 332 BC, the city is emptied of its people and resettled under a Macedonian garrison. Cyprus, whose kings joined Alexander during the siege, and the coast south to Gaza are now his. Egypt lies ahead, and its Persian satrap is already in contact with him. One city still resists: Gaza, the last town before the desert, where the commander Batis has hired Arab mercenaries and stored food for a long siege. Alexander marches straight down the coast.

Gaza stands on a high mound, and Alexander's engineers say that its wall cannot be taken by force. He raises a siege mound against the south wall all the same. A sortie nearly drives the Macedonians off it, and Alexander, leading his hypaspists to the rescue, is struck through the shoulder by a catapult bolt. The engines that took Tyre then arrive by sea. The walls are battered and undermined in the soft sand. Three assaults fail, and the fourth takes the city in autumn 332 BC, after a siege of about two months. Arrian writes that the defenders all die fighting and that their women and children are sold. Alexander resettles Gaza and makes it a fortress on the road to Egypt.

The fate of Batis is told by the Roman historian Curtius, who took it, the historian A. B. Bosworth thinks, from the Greek writer Cleitarchus. In his story Batis is taken alive, and Alexander has thongs passed through his ankles and drags him round the city behind a chariot, boasting that he follows the example of his ancestor Achilles. Arrian says nothing of it, but Bosworth holds that his silence does not prove the story false.
