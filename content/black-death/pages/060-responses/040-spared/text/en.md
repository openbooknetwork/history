In a few places the plague of 1348 seems to pass by. Historians still argue over why, and over how far the records can be trusted.

[Milan](@milan-visconti) is the largest city of northern Italy, ruled by Luchino Visconti and his brother Giovanni, the archbishop. Its chroniclers record few deaths in 1348. By a story told of Luchino, when the plague appears in three houses, he has their doors and windows walled up with the sick and the healthy inside, and leaves them to die. Samuel Cohn counts Milan among the cities that came through 1348 almost unharmed, and finds the reasons hard to explain. The evidence for this is mostly the silence of the chroniclers. In 1361 the plague returns and strikes Milan hard.

Poland is often said to have escaped because King Casimir III closed its borders. The story has no firm source, and such a ban would have been hard to enforce. Polish records of these years are too few to show how hard the plague struck [Kraków](@krakow), the king’s capital, or the rest of the kingdom. Bohemia, where Charles IV rules from [Prague](@prague), also left few records of the plague, and how hard it was struck is still debated.

Iceland is spared for half a century. The plague reaches Norway in 1349, but no ship brings it across the Atlantic. In 1402 a ship lands at [Hvalfjörður](@hvalfjordur-1402) in the west of Iceland, and the plague it carries kills perhaps half of the people of the island in the next two years.
