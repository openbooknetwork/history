The plague reaches [London](@london) in the autumn of 1348, and it is in the city by November. It comes in three ways: by road from Weymouth through Salisbury and Winchester, by road from Gloucester, and by ship along the coast. It kills through the winter and is worst in the spring of 1349.

In 1348 Ralph Stratford, the bishop of London, buys a field outside the walls at West Smithfield, called No Man’s Land, and encloses it with a brick wall for burials. In 1349 Sir Walter Manny, one of Edward III’s captains, rents 13 acres beside it for a second cemetery, where the Charterhouse is later built. Robert of Avesbury, a clerk in London, wrote that from 2 February to Easter, 12 April 1349, more than two hundred bodies were buried almost every day in the new cemetery at Smithfield. East of the Tower a clerk, John Corey, gets ground at [East Smithfield](@east-smithfield) for another burial ground, and in 1350 Edward III founds the abbey of St Mary Graces on it.

Edward III has called Parliament to meet at [Westminster](@westminster) on 19 January 1349. Early in January he puts it off until 28 April, because of a “terrible pestilence of vast deadliness, daily increasing in the city of London and neighbouring parts”.

Between 1986 and 1988 archaeologists of the Museum of London excavated part of the East Smithfield cemetery, before new buildings went up on the site of the old Royal Mint. They found the dead laid side by side in long trenches, in places five deep, about 760 of them by one count, and the museum keeps the remains of more than 600. In 2011 geneticists of the universities of Tübingen and McMaster recovered the DNA of the bacterium Yersinia pestis from the teeth of these dead. From it they pieced together the first draft genome of the bacterium that caused the Black Death, and showed that it is ancestral to most of the strains that cause plague today.
