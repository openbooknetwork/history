In 1407 the King of France is Charles VI, mad at intervals since 1392, as Lang dates it. Two princes of his blood fight over the power he cannot hold: his only brother, Louis, Duke of Orléans, and his cousin John the Fearless, Duke of Burgundy, whose lands take in the cities of Flanders to the north and, to the east, both the duchy and the county of Burgundy.

On Sunday 20 November 1407 the two dukes make peace and take communion together. On the evening of Wednesday 23 November, Louis of Orléans leaves the house where he has dined with the queen, Isabeau of Bavaria, and rides off on his mule down the rue Vieille-du-Temple in [Paris](@paris-1407). Near the Porte Barbette armed men fall on him in the dark and cut him down. Monstrelet, a chronicler of the Burgundian side, counts about eighteen of them, who had lodged at an inn near the gate and had planned the killing for days.

John the Fearless soon admits to two of the princes that he gave the order, and then says so openly in the council. He withdraws to [Lille](@lille-1407), and later, Lowell writes, the great men of the kingdom gather with him to hear a priest praise the murder.

The killing turns the quarrel of two dukes into civil war. From 1410 the Orléans party is led by Bernard, Count of Armagnac, father-in-law of Louis's young son, Charles of Orléans, and its men become known as the Armagnacs. Against them stand the Burgundians.

The map shows the kingdom as the war begins: the Duke of Burgundy's lands to the north and east, the English in Calais and Gascony, Brittany under its own duke. The sources name these lands by province and draw no line, so every border on the maps of this chapter is approximate.
