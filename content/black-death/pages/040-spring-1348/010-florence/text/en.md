The plague reaches Florence from [Pisa](@pisa) and the coast in March 1348, and the chronicler Marchionne di Coppo Stefani has it last until September. Florence is counted among the largest cities of Europe. In the 1330s the chronicler [Giovanni Villani](@villani) put it at about 90,000 people, reckoning from the bread it needed each day, and historians today put it nearer 120,000.

Giovanni Boccaccio lives through the plague in Florence and describes it at the opening of the Decameron, which he writes in the years after. A swelling rises in the groin or the armpit, at times the size of an apple, then black or purple spots spread over the body, and most of the sick die within three days. Fear breaks the bonds of families, he writes, so that “fathers and mothers were found to abandon their own children, untended, unvisited, to their fate, as if they had been strangers.” He puts the dead at more than 100,000 between March and July, and adds that before the plague nobody would have thought the city held so many.

Stefani tells how trenches are dug deep at most of the churches. The dead go in on the night they die, and the next morning they are covered with earth. More bodies go on top, with a little more earth over them, “just like one puts layers of cheese in a lasagna,” Stefani writes.

Villani keeps his chronicle of the city to the end. His last chapter tells of the plague and breaks off in the middle of a sentence: “And this plague lasted till …” He dies of it in 1348 with the date unwritten, and his brother Matteo carries the chronicle on.
