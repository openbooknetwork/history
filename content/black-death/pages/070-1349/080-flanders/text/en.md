Gilles Li Muisis, abbot of St Martin’s at [Tournai](@tournai), is about 77 when the plague reaches his town. His chronicle runs to 1349. By his account the plague comes to Tournai in August 1349, in the weeks when the flagellants arrive, and lasts until November. The miniatures that Pierart dou Tielt paints for his chronicle show the townspeople carrying coffins to new graves.

The magistrates of Tournai see the plague as God’s punishment for sin. They ban gambling and order couples who live together unmarried to marry or to part. Their register of bans burned in the bombardment of the city in May 1940, and it is known from transcriptions made in the 19th century by the archivist Frédéric Hennebert. The historians Claire Billen and Marc Boone note that the town’s leaders stay in the city through the epidemic.

The plague reaches Hainaut and Flanders in the summer of 1349. Further north, in Brabant and Holland, its progress cannot be traced. Historians long held that the Low Countries escaped lightly. Joris Roosen and Daniel Curtis, using the death duties of the county of Hainaut, find that the Black Death killed many there, women more than men. In Holland the draining of the wetlands stops at this time, perhaps because fewer people now need new land.

In the autumn the plague reaches the French court. Bonne of Luxembourg, the wife of John, Duke of Normandy and heir to the throne, dies at [Maubuisson](@bonne-of-luxembourg) near Pontoise on 11 September 1349, aged 34. Queen Joan of Burgundy, the wife of Philip VI, dies in [Paris](@joan-of-burgundy) on 12 December 1349.
