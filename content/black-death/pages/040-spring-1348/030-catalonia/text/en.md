In spring 1348 the plague reaches the lands of King Peter IV of Aragon. It comes overland from Languedoc into Roussillon and [Perpignan](@perpignan). South of the Pyrenees it most likely comes by sea from Mallorca, where [Palma](@palma) has had it since the end of March. [Barcelona](@burning-barcelona) has it from the start of May and [Valencia](@valencia) later that month. At Valencia the rebel nobles of the Union hold the king prisoner, and Peter talks them into releasing him, since the plague puts his life at risk.

That spring brings the first killings of Jews. At [Toulon](@burning-toulon) in Provence, on the night of Palm Sunday, 13 to 14 April 1348, townsmen break into the Jewish street, plunder the houses and kill 40 people. In Barcelona on 17 May, a funeral procession for a plague victim turns into the Call, the Jewish quarter, and the crowd plunders houses and kills about 20 people, by the count of the 16th-century Jewish chronicler Joseph ha-Kohen. The town council and the bishop stop the attack, and on 22, 24 and 27 May the king orders an inquiry and the punishment of the guilty. In July the killing spreads inland to [Tàrrega](@burning-tarrega), where 300 Jews die by Joseph ha-Kohen’s count. From 2007 archaeologists have dug up six mass graves at the edge of the town, which they link to the attack.

At Lleida, in April 1348, before the plague arrives, [Jacme d’Agramont](@agramont), a master of medicine at the town’s university, writes a short guide for the town councillors. His Regiment de preservació is the earliest dated tract on the plague of 1348 and is counted the first medical work composed in Catalan. It blames the disease on the stars and, it is said, on corrupted air, and urges the town to clear away its filth. Agramont dies at Lleida in 1348 or 1349, among the first the plague kills there.
