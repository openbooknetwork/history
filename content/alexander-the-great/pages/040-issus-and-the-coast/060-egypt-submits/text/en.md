Late in 332 BC Alexander leaves [Gaza](@gaza-332b) for Egypt. The road crosses about 200 km of inhospitable desert, and on the seventh day the army reaches [Pelusium](@pelusium-332), the fortress at the eastern edge of the Nile Delta. His fleet, sailing from Phoenicia, is already in its harbour. Pelusium had held off Persian invasions for much of the 4th century BC, but now no one resists. Egyptians gather there in crowds to greet Alexander as a liberator. Diodorus explains that the Persians had wronged their temples and ruled them harshly.

The last Persian satrap of Egypt, Mazaces, cannot fight. His predecessor died at Issus, and Arrian says that after Darius's defeat Mazaces has no Persian force left, so he lets Alexander into the country as a friend. Alexander puts a garrison into Pelusium and sends the fleet up the Nile. He himself leads the army through the desert to [Heliopolis](@heliopolis-332), keeping the river on his right, and then crosses to [Memphis](@memphis-332), the capital. There Mazaces hands over the city and its treasury.

At Memphis Alexander sacrifices to the gods, among them Apis, the sacred bull of the city, and holds athletic and music contests in the Greek manner, with the most famous performers of Greece. The historian A. B. Bosworth sees the sacrifice to Apis as a pointed contrast: Persian kings, Artaxerxes III among them, were said to have killed Apis bulls. In the winter of 332–331 BC Egypt passes to Alexander without a battle.
