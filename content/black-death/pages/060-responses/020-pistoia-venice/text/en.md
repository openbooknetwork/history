Two Italian towns write down rules against the plague in the spring of 1348, and their texts survive. Both act on the belief of the time that the plague spreads through foul air, and through the sick and their belongings.

On 30 March 1348 the Great Council of [Venice](@venice-savi) elects three men to watch over the public health. Days later they have deep pits dug on islands of the lagoon for the dead of the hospitals and the poor, with boats kept ready to carry the bodies out. By one account the islands are San Marco in Boccalama and San Leonardo in Fossamala. The rich leave the city, officials cannot be found, and too few councillors are left for the Great Council to do business. In July those who remain forbid Venetians to leave, on pain of losing their offices and rank.

[Pistoia](@pistoia-ordinances), a town of Tuscany between [Florence](@florence) and [Lucca](@lucca), issues its ordinances on 2 May 1348 and revises them on 23 May. Its citizens may not travel to [Pisa](@pisa) or Lucca, where the plague is raging, and no one from those towns may enter, a ban the revision lifts. Used cloth, of linen or wool, may not be brought in. The dead are to be carried in wooden coffins with nailed lids and buried two and a half arm’s lengths deep, about 1.8 metres. Those who come to a burial may walk with the body only as far as the church, and may not go back to the house. The cathedral’s bells may not ring for the dead, and a parish bell only once and softly, so that the sick do not lose heart. To spare needless costs, no one but the widow may wear new clothes in mourning. Butchers and tanners get new rules to keep foul smells out of the streets.

Neither town keeps the plague out. These rules are among the oldest public health measures of a town government that survive, and the health offices, quarantine and isolation hospitals of later centuries grew from them. Venice made its health office permanent in 1486.
