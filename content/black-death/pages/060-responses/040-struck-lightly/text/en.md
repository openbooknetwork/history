In a few lands the plague of 1348 and 1349 strikes lightly, or less than it strikes their neighbours. Among them are Milan, Poland, Bohemia and the border of France and Spain in the Pyrenees. Records from these places are few, and historians dispute how lightly.

[Milan](@milan-visconti), a city of nearly 100,000 people, is ruled by Luchino Visconti and his brother Giovanni, the archbishop. Matteo Villani, a chronicler of Florence, writes that in 1348 the plague weighs little on Milan and on some places near the Alps. A story told of Milan’s rulers has them board up the first houses struck, with their people inside. In 1361 the plague returns and strikes Milan hard.

Casimir III, the king of Poland, is often said to have kept the plague out by closing the borders of his kingdom. Only modern accounts tell this, and such a ban would have been hard to enforce. The plague reaches Poland and [Kraków](@krakow), the king’s capital, all the same, and strikes them less hard than the lands to the west. Bohemia, where Charles IV rules from [Prague](@prague), is reached as well and struck lightly, although no count of its dead survives.

Norway is struck in 1349, but Iceland stays free of the plague, and its annals tell of it only in Norway and abroad. The plague reaches the island in the autumn of 1402, by ship, by one annal at [Hvalfjörður](@hvalfjordur-1402) in the south-west, and lasts until Easter 1404. Gunnar Karlsson, a historian of Iceland, puts the dead at 50 to 60 percent of the people.
