Joan lodges in [Jacques Boucher's house](@boucher-house), the home of the Duke of Orléans's treasurer. The English have ringed the city with separate forts, the bastilles, on both banks of the Loire, but left the north-east open. North of the river stand [the bastille of Saint-Laurent](@bastille-saint-laurent), the largest, where the chief captains lie; the bastille of London, the bastille of Rouen and [the bastille of Paris](@bastille-paris); and [Saint-Loup](@saint-loup-april-1429) to the east. On the south bank are Saint-Jean-le-Blanc, [the Augustins](@augustins-april-1429), Saint-Privé and [the Tourelles](@tourelles-april-1429), with a fort on the Île Charlemagne between. Suffolk commands the English for Bedford, with Lord Talbot and Lord Scales, and William Glasdale holds the Tourelles.

On Saturday 30 April the captains La Hire and Florent d'Illiers drive the English back from their watch at Saint-Pouair, by the bastille of Paris; Joan takes no part. The English, who have kept the herald who brought her letter, send him back with word that they will burn her, and that she should go back to her cows. That evening, from [the Belle-Croix](@belle-croix), the French earthwork on the broken bridge facing the Tourelles, she calls on Glasdale to yield, promising him and his men their lives. They shout back that she is a cowherd.

On 1 May the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois), who commands in the city, rides to Blois to bring the army back, and on 2 May Joan rides out to look at the English forts. The army leaves Blois on 3 May by the north bank, through the Beauce. On the morning of 4 May Joan rides out to meet it with about 500 men, and it comes in past the English bastilles, whose garrisons stay inside. There are now about 5,000 regular troops in the city, and several thousand armed townsmen.
