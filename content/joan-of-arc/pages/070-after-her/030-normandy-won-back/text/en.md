In May 1444 England and France agree a general truce at Tours, where Margaret of Anjou is betrothed to Henry VI by proxy on 24 May.\* On 24 March 1449 the truce is broken. English soldiers from the garrisons of Normandy under François de Surienne seize and sack [Fougères](@fougeres-1449) in Brittany. French captains take Pont-de-l'Arche, Conches and Gerberoy in reprisal, and Charles VII's armies march into Normandy.

On 19 October 1449 the people of [Rouen](@rouen-1449) rise. Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset, the English lieutenant in Normandy, gives up the city by terms signed on 29 October, and on 10 November Charles VII makes his entry. When the year ends, Ramsay counts nine places left to the English: Fresnay, Domfront, Vire, Falaise, Honfleur, [Caen](@caen-1450), Bayeux, Saint-Sauveur and [Cherbourg](@cherbourg-1450). The map draws the English lands round these towns. Maine, English on the map of 1436, is the king's on this one, all but Fresnay.

About 15 March 1450 Sir Thomas Kyriell lands at Cherbourg with the last English relief army for Normandy. He takes Valognes, is joined by men of the garrisons under Matthew Gough, and on 14 April crosses the Fords of Saint-Clément below Carentan. John, Count of Clermont, the Duke of Bourbon's son, commanding in Lower Normandy, follows from Carentan by Isigny, and Arthur de Richemont, the Constable, comes from Saint-Lô.

They meet at Formigny on 15 April.\*\* The English stand with a stream and orchards behind them and their archers behind stakes in front. After about three hours of skirmishing the French bring up two culverins, and English archers rush out and seize them. Richemont's arrival decides the battle.\*\*\* Kyriell is taken, and Gough escapes to Bayeux. Oman and Beaucourt give 3,774 English dead, counted by the French heralds.\*\*\*\* How many French fell is not known, though their chroniclers name only about a dozen.

Caen agrees on 24 June to surrender. On 1 July Richemont escorts Somerset out and Dunois, once the Bastard of Orléans, takes the town. Cherbourg, the last English stronghold in Normandy, gives up on 12 August 1450.

### Notes

\* Ramsay has the truce signed four days after the betrothal, on 28 May. The text of the truce in Monstrelet is dated at Tours on 20 May.

\*\* Monstrelet's continuator dates the battle 18 April.

\*\*\* The accounts differ on the English line and on Richemont's attack. Oman puts a flank guard of men-at-arms under Gough beyond the English left and brings Richemont, with 1,200 men, onto the English flank. Ramsay rests the English right on a bridge and has Richemont send a detachment to his left to join Clermont while he advances on the English centre. Monstrelet's continuator has the constable's archers attack the English left wing at a bridge, and Kyriell fall back to the stream and the village.

\*\*\*\* Oman gives some 4,500 English, of whom Kyriell brought at most 2,500, against Clermont's 3,000 or so with two guns and Richemont's 1,200. Ramsay gives Kyriell 2,500, with perhaps 1,000 more from the garrisons, and Beaucourt gives Kyriell four to five thousand. The heralds' report in Monstrelet's continuator sets no more than 3,000 French against six to seven thousand English. Monstrelet's continuator counts 3,773 dead, buried in fourteen pits, and Beaucourt 1,200 to 1,400 prisoners. Ramsay thinks the English reckoning of 3,000 dead an exaggeration.
