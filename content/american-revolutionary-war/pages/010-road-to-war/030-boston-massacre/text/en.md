In 1767 Chancellor Townshend tries again with duties on glass, paint, paper, lead and tea, collected at the ports. The colonies answer with another boycott and with pamphlets arguing that Parliament may regulate trade but may not raise revenue in America. Boston is the centre of resistance. From 1 October 1768 four British regiments arrive to keep order in a town of about sixteen thousand.

Soldiers and townspeople clash for a year and a half. On the evening of 5 March 1770 a crowd throws snowballs and stones at a sentry outside the Custom House. The soldiers sent to support him fire, and five men die. Henry Pelham’s engraving of the shooting, copied and sold by Paul Revere as “The Bloody Massacre”, circulates through the colonies.

On the same day in London, Lord North proposes to repeal the Townshend duties except the one on tea. The repeal becomes law on 12 April 1770.
