The chronicles call the warriors of Osman and Orhan gazis or alps, horsemen of the frontier, the uç, who raid Byzantine lands for booty and fame. Aşıkpaşazade, writing in the late 15th century, counts four groups among the Turks of Anatolia: the gazis, the akhis, the abdals, who are wandering dervishes, and the bacıs, the sisters. In 1938 Paul Wittek argued that holy war against the Christians, gaza, built the Ottoman state. Since the 1980s most historians have rejected this view, as the early Ottomans also fought Muslim neighbours, took Christians into their service and used gaza for any raid.

By tradition Osman gives İnegöl to Turgut Alp, and Orhan’s commanders keep the lands they take: Kocaeli is the land of Akça Koca, who is buried at [Kandıra](@kandira), [Konuralp](@konuralp) is named after Konur Alp, and Abdurrahman Gazi holds [Aydos](@aydos). Dervishes settle among the warriors. Sheikh Edebali keeps a lodge at [Bilecik](@edebali-bilecik), and Geyikli Baba, a dervish said to ride a deer, joins the siege of Bursa and receives from Orhan the site of a lodge at [Babasultan](@geyikli-baba) below Uludağ. The chronicles name other dervishes of Orhan’s time, among them Abdal Musa, whose lodge at Elmalı in the south later becomes a centre of the Bektaşi order.

The akhis are brotherhoods of craftsmen, each led by a master called the akhi, who keep lodges and feed travellers. Ibn Battuta travels through Anatolia in the early 1330s and stays in akhi lodges in more than 25 towns. He visits Bursa and İznik, newly taken by Orhan, and goes on by Mudurnu and Bolu toward Kastamonu. In towns without a prince, such as Ankara, the akhis keep order.
