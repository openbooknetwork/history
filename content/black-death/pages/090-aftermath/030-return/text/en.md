The plague comes back. In England the second pestilence strikes in 1361 and 1362, falling hardest on infants and young men, and it kills perhaps a fifth of the people. Chroniclers across Europe call the waves from 1361 on plagues of children, it is said because they kill so many of the young born since 1348. [Henry of Grosmont](@henry-of-grosmont), Duke of Lancaster and one of Edward III’s leading commanders, dies at Leicester on 23 March 1361, possibly of the plague. By one recent study it is this second wave, more than the Black Death itself, that does lasting harm to the population and economy of England.

In 1361 the plague also strikes the papal court at [Avignon](@avignon-1361). A life of Pope Innocent VI counts about 6,000 dead there between Easter and 25 July, among them about a hundred bishops and eight or nine cardinals. In Tuscany it kills a fifth of the people of Pisa, Pistoia and Florence between 1361 and 1363.

A third wave follows in 1369, and in London and Tuscany it kills between a tenth and a seventh of the people. Another comes in 1374. Each kills fewer than the Black Death, and together they are one reason that Europeans stay fewer than they were before 1348 until the 1500s.

Rulers try harder to keep the sick apart from the healthy. In January 1374 Bernabò Visconti, lord of Milan, orders that the plague sick of [Reggio Emilia](@reggio-1374) leave the town for the open fields, where they either get well or die. Three years later Ragusa goes further.
