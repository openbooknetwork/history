[Châlons](@chalons-1429) opens to Charles VII: its bishop and burgesses come out and give him full obedience, and Perceval de Cagny, a chronicler of the Duke of Alençon's household, has him received there on 14 July. He lodges at [Sept-Saulx](@sept-saulx-1429), a castle of Regnault de Chartres, Archbishop of Reims, four leagues from the city. The Anglo-Burgundian captains leave Reims, and its burgesses bring him the keys. On Saturday 16 July the king rides into [Reims](@reims-cathedral), as Cagny tells it and as three gentlemen of Anjou wrote from the city the next day.

On Sunday 17 July the archbishop anoints and crowns Charles VII in the cathedral, in a service that runs from nine to two, the gentlemen wrote. The abbot of Saint-Remi brings the Holy Ampulla, escorted by four captains who ride armed into the church. Alençon knights the king and stands in for the absent Duke of Burgundy.

All through the rite, the gentlemen wrote, Joan stands beside the king with her standard in her hand. Asked by her judges in 1431 why her standard went into the church rather than the other captains', she answered: "It had been present in the perils; that was reason enough for it to be honored." The Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of Orléans, has her kneel in tears after the anointing and say that God's will is done. Lowell writes that whether she said it cannot be known.

That day Joan writes to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, bidding him make peace with the king. He does not answer. His envoys come to the king at Reims,\* and Ramsay has them get Georges de La Trémoille, the king's favourite, to sign a truce of fifteen days. Joan is told that the duke will hand over Paris when it ends. Bedford, the English regent, later counted Reims, Troyes and Châlons among the cities that went over without a fight.
