The Burgundian party made Pierre Cauchon Bishop of [Beauvais](@beauvais-1431) in 1420. In August 1429 the town went over to Charles VII and drove him out, and he has lived at Rouen since. With Beauvais in French hands, his trial of Joan sits at Rouen, under the English. His claim to judge her rests on her capture at [Compiègne](@compiegne-diocese-1431), which the record places in his diocese.\* The chapter of Rouen, which governs the church there while the archbishopric is vacant, lets him hold his court in its diocese. On 3 January 1431 Henry VI hands her over to him, keeping the right to take her back if she is not convicted.

She is held in a tower of [the castle of Rouen](@rouen-castle-1431), whose captain is Richard Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick. A church court tries her, yet she is kept in this secular prison, as Martin Ladvenu, a Dominican of the court, testified in 1450. Jean Massieu, the usher of the court, said that three English guards spent each night in her room.\*\*

Cauchon opens the trial on Tuesday 9 January 1431. Thurston writes that the assessors are nearly all French, and that most come from the University of [Paris](@university-of-paris-1431) as theologians and doctors. Lowell counts forty-three at the first public session. The Dominican Jean Le Maistre, the Inquisitor's deputy for the diocese of Rouen, refuses on 20 February to sit without special authority, unsure that his commission covers the case. Under a commission from the Inquisitor of France, Jean Graverent, he sits from 13 March as the second judge.

The English crown pays: each Paris doctor draws twenty sous tournois a day, and on 24 April Jean Le Maistre is given twenty saluts d'or for his attendance. She is summoned as "vehemently suspected of heresy". In Thurston's judgment the English could not put her to death for defeating them, but a church court could condemn her as a witch and a heretic.

### Notes

\* Lang gives Canon Dunand's view that Compiègne lay in the diocese of Soissons, not of Beauvais.

\*\* Whether she was kept in an iron cage is told differently. Thurston says she was at first, chained by the neck, hands and feet. Lowell says it is not certain the cage was ever used, and Lang that one witness saw her in irons but not in the cage. Massieu said in 1450 that at night her legs were chained to a great beam of wood.
