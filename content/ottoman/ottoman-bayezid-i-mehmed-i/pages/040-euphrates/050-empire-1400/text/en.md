By 1400 Bayezid rules from the Danube to the Euphrates at [Malatya](@malatya-1399). He removes the dynasties he has defeated in Anatolia and sets out to build a centralised state run by his own men. Stefan Lazarević of Serbia and the other Christian vassals in the Balkans owe him tribute and troops, and his reliance on them angers the Anatolian beys and the Muslim scholars. Bayezid takes the title Sultan of Rûm, after the Arabic name for the lands of the Eastern Roman Empire, and has the Abbasid caliph in Cairo confirm it.

The state rests on the sultan’s household of slaves, the kul: men taken in war or bought, and since the 1380s boys levied from the Christian villages of the Balkans, raised as Muslims and trained for the palace or for the janissaries, the sultan’s infantry. An early account of the boy levy is a sermon that Isidore Glabas, the metropolitan of [Thessalonica](@thessalonica-1394), gives on 28 February 1395 on the children taken at the sultan’s order. The grand vizier Çandarlı Ali Pasha, in office since 1387, founds the corps of palace pages and sets the fees of the kadıs.

In [Bursa](@bursa-ulu-cami) Bayezid spends the spoils of Nicopolis on the Ulu Cami, built by the architect Ali Neccar between 1396 and 1399, a hall of twenty domes in four rows of five on twelve pillars. By tradition he had vowed twenty mosques for the victory and built one mosque with twenty domes instead. On the east side of the town stands his own complex, the Yıldırım complex, with a mosque, a medrese and a hospital. His castle of Güzelcehisar guards the Bosporus.
