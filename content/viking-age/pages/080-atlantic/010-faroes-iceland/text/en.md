Irish monks reach the islands of the northern ocean before the Norse. In 825 the Irish monk Dicuil, teaching in the Frankish empire, writes of islands two days’ sail north of Britain where hermits from Ireland had lived for nearly a hundred years until "Norwegian pirates" drove them out, leaving countless sheep. These are taken to be the Faroes. Dicuil also reports clerics who spent a summer around 795 on Thule, possibly Iceland, where at midsummer the light is bright enough to pick lice from a shirt.

Burnt barley and sheep DNA show that people lived on the Faroes centuries before the Norse, who settle there in the 9th century. Færeyinga saga names Grímur Kamban as the first settler, at Funningur on Eysturoy by local tradition. His Gaelic byname suggests that he came by way of the Norse settlements in Ireland or the Hebrides.

Landnámabók tells how Iceland was found. Naddodd, sailing from Norway to the Faroes, is driven off course to the east coast, climbs a mountain near Reyðarfjörður, sees no smoke from any hearth and names the land Snæland, Snowland. About 860 the Swede Garðar Svavarsson, blown north by a storm in the Pentland Firth, sails round the island, winters at Húsavík and names it Garðarshólmi.

About 868 Flóki Vilgerðarson sails from western Norway by Shetland and the Faroes with three ravens. The third raven flies north-west and leads him to land, and after a hard winter at Vatnsfjörður he sees a fjord full of drift ice and names the land Ísland. Ari Þorgilsson and Landnámabók write that the first settlers found Irish monks, the papar, who left when the Norse came and left behind books, bells and croziers. Place names such as Papey keep their memory, and caves at Seljaland in the south, with crosses of Irish style and traces of people from about 800, may be their work.
