In July 1806 Napoleon founds the Confederation of the Rhine, a league of German states under his protection, and Emperor Francis II dissolves the Holy Roman Empire. In peace talks with Britain Napoleon offers to give back Hanover, which he had promised to Prussia months before. The war party at the Prussian court, led by Queen Louise, gains the upper hand, and on 7 August King Frederick William III decides on war. His only German ally is Saxony, and the Russian army is still far to the east.

Prussia mobilises 171,000 men, 20,000 of them Saxons, in three armies under the Duke of Brunswick, Prince Hohenlohe, and Ernst von Rüchel with Gebhard von Blücher. Brunswick’s orders must be confirmed by the king, and councils of war debate several plans for weeks. On 5 October, when a reconnaissance finds the French moving north from Bayreuth, they decide to gather Hohenlohe’s army at Rudolstadt, Brunswick’s at Erfurt and Rüchel’s at Gotha. An ultimatum demands that the French withdraw west of the Rhine by 8 October, and on 9 October Prussia declares war.

Napoleon calls up 50,000 conscripts on 5 September and gathers the Grande Armée, about 180,000 men, along the southern border of Saxony. On 8 October it crosses the Franconian Forest in three columns close enough to support each other, an order of march he calls the bataillon carré. Soult’s and Ney’s corps march by Hof on the right, Bernadotte’s and Davout’s with Murat’s cavalry and the Guard by Schleiz in the centre, and Lannes’s and Augereau’s by Saalfeld on the left. By Petre’s count the French advance on a front of 60 km, while the Prussian and Saxon army is spread over 145 km.

On 9 October Drouet’s division of Bernadotte’s corps and Murat’s cavalry drive Bogislav von Tauentzien’s Prussians and Saxons out of Schleiz, and Tauentzien falls back to Auma with a loss of 566 men. The next day at Saalfeld, Lannes with 12,800 men meets the 8,300 Prussians and Saxons of Prince Louis Ferdinand, the advance guard of Hohenlohe’s army. While the 17th Light Infantry holds the front, Suchet’s division marches through the woods around the open Prussian right.

At 11 a.m. an order from Hohenlohe tells the prince to stay at Rudolstadt, and he tries to break off the fight. Just before 1 p.m. Lannes attacks along the whole line. The prince leads his cavalry against the French hussars and chasseurs and is overwhelmed, and near Wöhlsdorf he refuses to surrender and is cut down by Quartermaster Guindet of the 10th Hussars. His force loses about 900 killed and wounded, 1,800 prisoners and 33 guns, the French 172 to 200 men.
