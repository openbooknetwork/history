Charles VII stays at Reims until Thursday 21 July.\* Then he rides to the shrine of Saint-Marcoul at [Corbeny](@corbeny-1429) and touches the sick for the King's Evil, as kings did after their anointing. There the keys or the deputies of [Laon](@laon-1429) are brought to him. He goes on by [Vailly](@vailly-1429) on 22 July, and on Saturday 23 July he enters [Soissons](@soissons-1429), which has sent him its keys.

The road to Paris lies open, but the army turns south instead. Ramsay blames Georges de La Trémoille, the king's favourite. Beaucourt puts it down to the wish to spare the Duke of Burgundy, who seemed well disposed. [Château-Thierry](@chateau-thierry-1429), held for Burgundy by the lords of Châtillon, Croy and Brimeu, gives itself up: its townsmen lean to the king, Monstrelet says, and its lords expect no help soon. The king enters on the evening of 29 July and stays until 1 August. There on 31 July, at Joan's request, he frees Domrémy and Greux, her home villages, from taxes, by the grant that Lang and Lowell cite. [Crécy](@crecy-1429) and [Coulommiers](@coulommiers-1429) declare for him as well. On Tuesday 2 August he reaches [Provins](@provins-1429), where he stays until Friday 5 August, Perceval de Cagny writes.

Bedford, regent of France for Henry VI, has meanwhile had fresh troops from England, brought by Cardinal Beaufort. Ramsay counts 250 spears and 2,500 bows entering Paris on 25 July, and Monstrelet about 4,000 fighting men. On the last day of July the poet Christine de Pizan finishes her Ditié, a poem in praise of Joan, as its closing lines say.

Bedford's memorandum of December 1433, as Lang dates it, names Laon, [Sens](@sens-1429) and Provins among the cities lost after Orléans.

The map shows the king's road from Reims to Provins, and the land between the Marne and the Aisne gone from the English zone. Both are approximate. Paris stays English, and whether the king will march on it is still undecided.
