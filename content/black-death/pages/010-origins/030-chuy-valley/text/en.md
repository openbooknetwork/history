The Chüy valley runs along the foot of the Tian Shan, near today’s Bishkek and Tokmok in Kyrgyzstan. In the 13th and 14th centuries a Christian community of the Church of the East lives here, on the caravan road to China. About two in three of the names on its graves are Turkic. Its dead lie under stones carved with a cross and an inscription in Syriac script, dated by the Seleucid era and the Turkic years of the twelve animals. By one account the stones are boulders taken from the rivers.

In 1885 a Russian surveyor and a local doctor come upon two of its cemeteries, at [Kara-Djigach](@kara-djigach) near Bishkek and at [Burana](@burana) near Tokmok, west of [Lake Issyk-Kul](@issyk-kul). Digs from 1885 to 1892 bring up 581 headstones, and in 1888 thirty-three skulls are sent to St Petersburg. The orientalist Daniel Chwolson publishes the inscriptions from 1886 to 1897.

Of the 467 stones that carry a date, 118 fall in the two years from October 1337 to September 1339. Ten of these give the cause of death as mawtānā, Syriac for pestilence. In 2019 the historian Philip Slavin read the sudden rise as a local outbreak of plague, and left open whether it led to the Black Death.

In 2022 a team led by Maria Spyrou, Philip Slavin and Johannes Krause read the DNA in teeth of seven of the dead from both cemeteries. Three of the Kara-Djigach dead carried Yersinia pestis. Their strain, which the team dates to 1338–1339, sits at the root of the family tree of the strains that spread in the Black Death. Its closest living relatives are found in marmots of the Tian Shan. The authors conclude that the Black Death arose in this region in the early 14th century.

The stones do not show how the plague left the valley. Eight years pass before the sources find it again, on the Volga.
