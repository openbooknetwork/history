By June 1348 the plague has spread from the ports and from Tuscany into the towns of the Po valley, among them [Padua](@padua) and [Bologna](@bologna). At Bologna it strikes the university, and Giovanni d’Andrea, its best known teacher of canon law, dies of it on 7 July. A will needed a notary, and the city’s registers show 185 notaries writing wills in July, against 85 in June and 89 in August. The historian Shona Kelly Wray has used these registers to follow the plague through the city’s streets and families.

Petrarch, the poet, is at [Parma](@parma). On the morning of 19 May a letter from his friend Ludovico tells him that Laura, the woman of his poems, died at Avignon on 6 April. He writes the news in Latin on a fly-leaf of his copy of Virgil. He first saw her in the church of Santa Clara at Avignon on 6 April 1327, he notes, and on the same day in 1348 “that light was taken from our day, while I was by chance at Verona, ignorant, alas! of my fate”, in the translation of James Harvey Robinson and Henry Rolfe. The book is now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana at [Milan](@milan).

In the north-east the plague crosses the Alps. Carinthia is still clearing the ruins of the earthquake of 25 January 1348, which wrecked [Villach](@villach) and brought down a mountainside on the villages below. Later in 1348 the plague comes from [Venice](@venice) through Friuli and over the passes into Carinthia and Styria, by one account.

Milan, said to be the largest city of the region, is struck only lightly in 1348.
