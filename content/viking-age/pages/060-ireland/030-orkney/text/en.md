Norse settlers come to Orkney and Shetland in the late 8th and early 9th centuries, and the Picts of the islands lose their land to them. Whether this happens in peace or by force is debated. The Norse call Orkney and Shetland Norðreyjar, the Northern Isles, and the Hebrides and Man Suðreyjar, the Southern Isles.

The Orkneyinga saga, written down in Iceland in the early 13th century, tells that Harald Fairhair, king of Norway, takes the Northern Isles about 875 and gives them as an earldom to Rognvald of Møre. Rognvald passes them to his brother Sigurd the Mighty, earl until about 892, who wins Caithness and Sutherland on the Scottish mainland together with Thorstein the Red. Rognvald’s son Torf-Einarr, earl from about 895 to 910, founds the line of earls that rules Orkney for centuries. Some historians think the story of Harald’s voyage is modelled on the expeditions of Magnus Barefoot two centuries later.

For the Hebrides and Man the sources are mostly Irish annals and sagas. The Norse probably hold Man by 900, and Amlaíb Cuarán, king of Dublin, dies in religious retirement on Iona in 980 or 981. On Christmas night 986 Danes sack Iona and kill the abbot and fifteen elders of the monastery. In 987 Gofraid son of Harald, king of the Isles, wins the battle of Man.

Sigurd the Stout, earl from about the 980s, holds Orkney, Shetland and Caithness, and from about 990 the Hebrides, where he places a jarl named Gilli. By the Orkneyinga saga his mother makes him a raven banner that brings victory to the army it goes before and death to the man who carries it, and with it he defeats Findláech of Moray. The saga tells that Olaf Tryggvason, sailing from Dublin to Norway in 995, makes Sigurd accept baptism at South Walls. The Annals of Ulster record Sigurd’s death at Clontarf in 1014, the earliest mention of the earldom of Orkney.
