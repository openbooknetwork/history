In the winter of 1347–1348 the plague spreads west from the Rhône into Languedoc. Louis Heyligen, writing from Avignon in April 1348, tells of the galley driven out of Marseille. It joins the two other galleys driven from Genoa, and they sail for Spain, spreading the sickness in the ports along their way. On land the plague travels the Via Domitia, the road the Romans built from Italy to Spain, which by one account still carries the traffic between Provence, Languedoc and Catalonia. The road runs past [Montpellier](@montpellier) and [Béziers](@beziers) to [Narbonne](@narbonne). There it divides. One branch leads south to [Perpignan](@perpignan) and Catalonia, the other west to [Carcassonne](@carcassonne) and Toulouse.

The dates do not follow the road. Carcassonne has the plague in January 1348, and Montpellier, Béziers, Narbonne and Perpignan by March. At Narbonne a reported 30,000 people die. By 27 April, Heyligen writes, it has reached as far as Toulouse.

Montpellier has one of the great medical schools of Europe, and its masters, it is said, are among the first to write about the new sickness. One of them, who does not give his name, writes in 1349 that it passes through the air from the eyes of the sick to anyone who looks at them.

Perpignan is the chief town of Roussillon, which King Peter IV of Aragon took from the king of Mallorca in 1344. The historian Richard Emery has read the registers of its notaries, who wrote down the town’s loans, sales and wills. Moneylenders make 25 loans in February 1348, but after mid-April they make almost none until August. Of about 125 notaries and scribes at work before the plague, only about 45 appear in the records after it. Most of the town’s physicians vanish from the records too.
