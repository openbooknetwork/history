The plague crosses the Alps into Carinthia and Styria by November 1348. The chronicle of Neuberg Abbey in Styria, the chief witness for Austria, dates its outbreak to St Martin’s day, 11 November 1348. In May 1349 the plague reaches [Vienna](@vienna), the city of Albert II, Duke of Austria, and it stays until September. About a third of the city dies, by one estimate. The calendar of Zwettl Abbey records 500 to 600 deaths a day there, and 960 on a single day. Burial in the churchyards is forbidden, and the dead are carried out of the city to pits. Albert leaves Vienna with his court and family, as do others who can.

Albert II punishes those of his towns that attack their Jews. By one account the Jewish communities of Austria are among the few in the Empire that survive these years.

In Hungary 1349 is the year the plague strikes hardest. The wife of King Louis I, [Margaret of Bohemia](@margaret-of-bohemia), a daughter of Charles IV, dies of it on 7 September 1349, aged about 14, at Visegrád on the Danube, it is said.

By tradition the first German-speaking town the plague strikes is Mühldorf am Inn, on 29 June 1348. At Regensburg in 1349 Konrad of Megenberg, a canon of the cathedral, is writing his Book of Nature, which its editor calls the first natural history in German. Megenberg doubts that the Jews poisoned the wells, because so many Jews of Vienna died of the plague that they had to enlarge their cemetery. In the west the plague moves from Lake Geneva to the Swiss towns. It reaches [Bern](@bern) and Zürich in 1349, and [Basel](@basel) in April or May, three or four months after Basel has burned its Jews.
