Genoese traders hold Caffa on the Black Sea, where the plague first met the trade of Italy, and every autumn the galley fleets of [Genoa](@genoa) sail home from the Black Sea to the Ligurian coast.

A letter written at Avignon in April 1348 by Louis Heyligen, a Flemish musician in a cardinal’s household, tells of three galleys from the east that come into Genoa on the last day of 1347, loaded with spices and with dying men. Whoever trades with them dies, so the Genoese drive them out of the harbour with burning arrows. They act too late, because the plague is already in the city.

In January 1348 the plague reaches [Pisa](@pisa), down the coast in Tuscany. The Sienese chronicler Agnolo di Tura writes that it comes from Genoa. From Pisa it moves inland along the roads, to [Lucca](@lucca), about 20 km away, in February, and to [Pistoia](@pistoia) by the end of March. On land, it is said, it travels a few kilometres a day at most.

In the Camposanto, the walled cemetery beside Pisa cathedral, a fresco shows the Triumph of Death. Riders on a hunt come upon three open coffins, and Death with a scythe flies over a crowd of the rich. Historians long read it as a picture of the plague. In 1974 the art historian Luciano Bellosi gave it to the painter Buonamico Buffalmacco and dated it to about 1336–1341, years before the plague, and many now follow him.
