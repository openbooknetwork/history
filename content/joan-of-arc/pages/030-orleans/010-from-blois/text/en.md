On 27 or 28 April 1429 the convoy for besieged Orléans sets out from [Blois](@blois), its priests walking ahead and singing hymns.\* The marshals Gilles de Rais and de Boussac lead the escort, with the admiral de Culant, La Hire and Ambroise de Loré. Joan rides with them, brought here by Raoul de Gaucourt, governor of Orléans.

She wants to go straight through the Beauce, on the north bank, where the English are strongest. So the captains take the south bank through the Sologne, and do not tell her. On 28 April the convoy passes Olivet, opposite the city.\*\* The Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois), commanding in the city, crosses to meet her. In his account of 1456 she asks at once whether it was he who had her brought this way.

The supplies go by water: boats from the city are loaded upstream, before [Chécy](@checy), at the Île-aux-Bourdons, says the Orléans chronicle of the feast of 8 May.\*\*\* The contrary wind turns, and the laden boats sail down past the English bastilles while the garrison skirmishes before the English fort of Saint-Loup.

Joan crosses the river with about 200 lances, the figure of Simon Beaucroix, a squire of her company, and of Ramsay. The rest of the escort goes back to cross at Blois, with her confessor Jean Pasquerel and her banner. The Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of the city, has her company lie a night at Chécy.\*\*\*\*

At about eight in the evening of Friday 29 April she rides into [Orléans](@orleans-entry) armed, on a white horse, with the Bastard beside her and townspeople carrying torches. The English do nothing to stop her. She lodges with Jacques Boucher, the Duke of Orléans's treasurer, by the Porte Regnart.

The map shows the convoy's road, the boats and her ride in. These lines, and the places of the English forts on the next pages, are approximate: none of the sources this book draws on gives a plan of the siege.

### Notes

\* Anatole France gives 27 April, noting that the German chronicler Windecke has the 28th. Lowell gives the 28th, and Ramsay leaves it at one or the other. Lowell puts the army at about 3,000, and Lang finds the same number in Windecke and the Chronique de Tournai. Monstrelet gives 7,000, and Joan told her judges in 1431 that the king gave her 10,000 or 12,000 men. Lang calls the large figures impossible: such an army would soon have eaten the supplies it brought.

\*\* Lang, Anatole France and Ramsay give 28 April. Lowell has her reach the heights of Olivet about noon on Friday 29 April.

\*\*\* Dunois remembered the convoy coming to the river facing the church of Saint-Loup, and Ramsay follows him. Lowell has the boats taken five miles up the river from the city.

\*\*\*\* Lang and Anatole France have her sleep at Reuilly, the house of Guy de Cailly, and Ramsay at Rully near Chécy. Lowell takes the Journal's Chécy for a slip of the pen and reads Cléry, on the march.
