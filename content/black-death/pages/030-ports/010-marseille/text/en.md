In November 1347 ships from the east bring the plague to [Marseille](@marseille), a major port of Provence, the county ruled by Queen Joanna I of Naples. By one account the merchants of Marseille trade with Genoa, Sicily and the Levant, and the sickness comes in with their galleys.

From the coast the plague moves up the Rhône. [Arles](@arles) is struck very soon after Marseille, and most of its people die. By January 1348 the plague is in [Avignon](@avignon), about 85 km north of Marseille. On the road it takes about two months over that distance, less than 2 km a day. The ships’ course and the road drawn on the map are approximate.

Avignon has been the seat of the popes since 1309, and its streets are crowded with clergy, envoys, merchants and pilgrims. On 27 April 1348 Louis Heyligen, a Flemish musician in the household of Cardinal Giovanni Colonna, writes from the city to Bruges, and a later page gives his count of the dead of Avignon.

[Corsica](@corsica) and [Sardinia](@sardinia) lie on the sea lanes between Sicily and Genoa. Late in 1347 ships from Sicily call at both islands and leave the plague there. Corsica, it is said, belongs to Genoa, and most of Sardinia to the Crown of Aragon.
