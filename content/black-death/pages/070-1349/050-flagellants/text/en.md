In 1349 bands of penitents come out of Austria and Hungary and walk west from town to town behind crosses and banners, it is said by way of Bavaria and Franconia to the Rhine. Twice a day they strip to the waist in a square or a churchyard and beat themselves with whips tipped with iron. By one account a man who joins swears to obey the master of his band for 33 and a half days, one day for each year of Christ’s life. The chronicler Fritsche Closener describes their rites at [Strasbourg](@flagellants-strasbourg) that summer. By August they are in Flanders, and Gilles Li Muisis, abbot of St Martin’s at [Tournai](@flagellants-tournai), writes that on the feast of the Assumption, 15 August, “there came from the town of Bruges about 200 men.”

In late June 1349 Charles IV, king of the Romans, pledges the Jews of [Frankfurt](@burning-frankfurt) to the town and promises it impunity if they are killed. On 14 July they are killed, some 60 men and women by the records, and their houses burned. The flagellants enter [Mainz](@burning-mainz) in August. The Jews there kill several Christians in a clash, and when they can hold out no longer they burn themselves in their houses, on 24 August, one account has it. The Jews of [Cologne](@burning-cologne) do the same in the night of 23 to 24 August. At [Nuremberg](@burning-nuremberg), where Charles has allowed a new market square on the site of the Jewish quarter, at least 562 Jews are killed on 5 December, and Charles’s Frauenkirche rises over the ruins of their synagogue.

In most towns the flagellants arrive only after the killings. At Frankfurt the king, the nobles, the council and the guilds had planned the massacre, and elsewhere too men of rank stand behind the persecutions.

On 20 October 1349 Clement VI condemns the flagellants in a bull and orders the bishops to suppress them, and the movement fades within months. By 1351 most Jewish communities of the Empire have been destroyed, and many of the survivors move east to Poland, where Casimir III gives them refuge.
