The plague comes to England by sea. The chronicle of the Franciscan friars of King’s Lynn names [Melcombe](@melcombe) in Dorset as the first place in England to be infected. Two ships, one of them from Bristol, put in there shortly before the feast of St John the Baptist on 24 June 1348, and a sailor on board “had brought with him from Gascony the seeds of the terrible pestilence.” Melcombe Regis is today part of the town of Weymouth.

Gascony, the land around Bordeaux, belongs to the king of England, and every year English ships sail there for its wine. The plague is in Bordeaux by June 1348, and a ship carries it across the Bay of Biscay in about a week.

On land it moves at the pace of people and carts, along the roads from the Dorset coast. On 17 August Ralph of Shrewsbury, the bishop of Bath and Wells, orders processions every Friday in the churches of his diocese, to pray that God will turn the plague away from [Wells](@wells) and its people. At about the same time the plague reaches [Bristol](@bristol), by one account the second port of the kingdom. There, wrote Henry Knighton, a canon at Leicester, “almost the whole strength of the town” died, in the translation of the historian Francis Gasquet.

East of Dorset the plague moves along the road through Salisbury and [Winchester](@winchester) towards London. On 24 October William Edendon (or Edington), the bishop of Winchester, writes to his clergy that the plague has begun a cruel attack on the coasts of England, and orders prayers and processions. By the end of 1348 it has reached London.
