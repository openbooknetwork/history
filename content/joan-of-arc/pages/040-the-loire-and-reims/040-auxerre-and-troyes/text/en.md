At [Gien](@gien-rendezvous), where Joan and the Duke of Alençon join Charles VII on 24 June, the princes and captains want the king to go into Normandy, and others want the upper Loire cleared first. Joan holds out for Reims, and the council gives way. On Wednesday 29 June the king leaves Gien. He has no money to pay the army, says Perceval de Cagny, a chronicler of Alençon's household, and knights, squires and common men follow Joan unpaid. Friar Richard, a preacher lately driven out of Paris, goes with the army.\*

The road runs through towns held for the English and the Burgundians since about the Treaty of Troyes.\*\* [Auxerre](@auxerre-truce), a Burgundian town, keeps its gates shut but sells the army food under a truce, and promises to do as Troyes, Châlons and Reims do. After about three days the king moves on. The Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of Orléans, says the citizens secretly paid Georges de La Trémoille, the king's favourite, 2,000 écus to be spared an assault; Lang reports it as a thing that was said. [Saint-Florentin](@saint-florentin-1429) submits on the road beyond.

[Troyes](@troyes-1429) refuses to open, and its garrison, 500 to 600 English and Burgundians by the Journal, sallies out. Cagny has the king before the town on Friday 8 July.\*\*\* The army is short of food. At a council, the Journal says, the captains hear that it lacks artillery and money, and most think the town too strong. Robert le Maçon, of the king's council, asks that Joan be heard. She promises Troyes within two or three days, by love or by force, and makes ready an assault. The bishop and the townsmen come out to treat: the garrison may leave with its goods, and the town is pardoned. The Journal says that Joan would not let the garrison lead away its French prisoners, and that the king paid their ransoms. On Sunday 10 July Charles VII enters Troyes.\*\*\*\*
