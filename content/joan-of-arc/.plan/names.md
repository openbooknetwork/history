# Names of Joan of Arc

"First named in" gives the section that first names it and the digest claim, `research.md`. "Other forms" are the
spellings the story's source files use, in the OCR where the file is OCR. A text quotes them only inside a quote. A
name the digest gives only in a quote or a note is marked so. A name marked "not told" stands under a rule of
`rules.md` that leaves its claim out, and is listed only so that nobody looks for it again.

## Rules

### Calendar and dates

- Dates are in the Julian calendar, as the sources give them; none is turned into the Gregorian. The year begins
  on 1 January. A source that begins the year at Easter, the custom of France then, is turned: the Chronique de la
  Pucelle's Baugé "in 1420" is 22 March 1421, and Murray's "first enquiry, 1449" is March 1450.
- A day is written day, month, year: 8 May 1429. A feast day the sources name may follow once in a text: "8
  September 1429, the feast of the Nativity of Our Lady". The weekday only where the page turns on it: "on Sunday
  8 May the English drew up but no one fought".
- A day the sources give differently follows `rules.md`: the day the primary sources give, or most of the sources,
  with the author who differs named where the page turns on it; where the digest marks the day disputed and no
  account leads, the month. Her birth is "about 1412". The first voices are "about 1424 or 1425", or "at about
  thirteen" in her own words.
- A date known only by season or part of a month takes it: "in summer 1428", "early in June 1429", "at the end of
  March 1430". A winter across two years takes both: "in winter 1428–1429". A date given only as "before" a day
  keeps it: "taken by 9 November 1429".
- An approximate year, age or number takes "about": about 1412, about nineteen, about 3,000.
- Prose writes a span as "from 1407 to 1456"; a label writes "1407–1456", and a date range "29 April – 8 May
  1429", with an en dash.
- Money keeps the source's unit: livres tournois, francs, écus, saluts. The sale is "ten thousand livres tournois"
  in John of Luxembourg's version and "ten thousand francs" in Ramsay's, each with its author (`rules.md`).

### Names of places

- A place takes the name on today's maps of France, with its French accents, and the short form of it where today's
  commune adds a qualifier to the old name: Meung, Lagny, Sully, Crépy, Bray, Verneuil, Domrémy, Châlons, Selles,
  Castillon. Where the place had another name then, it follows once a page in brackets: "Clermont-Ferrand (then
  Clermont)".
- Places that English has its own name for keep it: Paris, Reims (not Rheims), Burgundy, Brittany, Normandy,
  Picardy, Flanders, Lorraine, Champagne, Gascony, Dauphiné, Mont-Saint-Michel. The battle of 1415 is Agincourt;
  the village, where a text names it as a place, is Azincourt.
- A battle, siege or treaty keeps the name histories give it: the Treaty of Troyes, the battle of Agincourt, the
  battle of Baugé, the battle of Cravant, the battle of Verneuil, the Battle of the Herrings (fought at Rouvray),
  the siege of Orléans and the relief of Orléans, the battle of Patay, the attack on Paris, the siege of Compiègne,
  the Congress of Arras and the Treaty of Arras, the Truce of Tours, the battle of Formigny, the battle of Castillon,
  the Hundred Years' War.
- The English siege works of Orléans are "bastilles", the earthworks before a gate or a fort "boulevards", and each
  bastille keeps "the bastille of" before its name, so that the bastilles of Paris, Rouen and London are never taken
  for the cities. The Bastille Saint-Antoine in Paris is "the Bastille", with a capital.
- The structures of Orléans and Rouen keep the French name where the sources and histories keep it (the Tourelles,
  the Augustins, the Belle-Croix, the Porte Regnart, the Porte Saint-Honoré) and the English one where the histories
  give one (the Old Market, the cemetery of Saint-Ouen, the castle of Rouen, the pig market).
- Places that share a name keep a fixed form: Mehun-sur-Yèvre always in full, apart from Meung; Saint-Denis the town
  and Saint Denis the saint, whose name was the war cry of France; Clermont-Ferrand the town and Clermont the count;
  Saint-Sauveur in Normandy as "Saint-Sauveur" and the church on the Old Market as "the church of Saint-Sauveur".

### Names of people

- A person takes the name of the time. The Dauphin Charles becomes Charles VII on 30 October 1422. The Bastard of
  Orléans becomes Dunois in 1439. John Talbot is Lord Talbot until 1442 and Earl of Shrewsbury after, and "Talbot"
  throughout. The Duke of Bourbon's son is Count of Clermont through the story.
- Kings keep their numerals at every mention: Charles VI, Charles VII, Henry V, Henry VI, Edward III. No text writes
  "Henry" or "Charles" alone.
- No man is "John" alone: John the Fearless, Bedford, John of Luxembourg, Alençon, Talbot, Fastolf, Darnley, each by
  the short form his row gives. "Philip" alone is not used: Philip the Good, or the Duke of Burgundy.
- A title alone means one person in each span: "the Duke of Burgundy" John the Fearless until 10 September 1419 and
  Philip the Good after; "the Duke of Alençon" the duke of 1424–1429; "the Duke of Orléans" Louis in 1407 and
  Charles after; "the Constable" Richemont from 1425; "the Archbishop of Reims" Regnault de Chartres in 020–060;
  "the regent" Bedford, 1422–1435; "Suffolk" William de la Pole; "Somerset" Edmund Beaufort in 070. The others who
  share a title keep the full form their row gives at every mention: John I, Duke of Alençon; the Constable
  d'Albret; Michael de la Pole, Earl of Suffolk; John Beaufort, Earl of Somerset; Edward, Duke of Bar.
- Three women named Jeanne at Beaurevoir keep their titles, the Demoiselle of Luxembourg and the Lady of Beaurevoir,
  so that the name stays with Joan. Two men called the Bastard keep their "of": the Bastard of Orléans, the Bastard
  of Wandonne.
- Princes, kings and the great lords with an English form in the histories take it: John the Fearless, Philip the
  Good, John, Duke of Bedford, John of Luxembourg, Louis of Luxembourg, Isabeau of Bavaria, Catherine of Valois,
  Yolande of Aragon, René of Anjou, Charles of Orléans. Other French and Burgundian men and women keep the French form
  with its particle: Jean de Metz, Robert de Baudricourt, Gilles de Rais, Raoul de Gaucourt, Georges de La Trémoille,
  Regnault de Chartres, Pierre Cauchon, Isabelle Romée, Arthur de Richemont. A short form drops the particle "de"
  and keeps "La" and "Le": Baudricourt, Gaucourt, La Trémoille, La Hire, Le Maistre.
- Saints written out in full: Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, Saint Margaret, Saint Denis, Saint John's Day.
- "Captain", "bailli", "marshal", "constable", "admiral", "duke" and "bishop" take a capital only as part of a title
  before or after a name: the Constable de Richemont, the Bishop of Beauvais, Marshal of France; "the bishop" after.

### Transliteration and spelling

- French names keep their accents in every text and label: Orléans, Domrémy, Compiègne, Montépilloy, Saint-Pierre-
  le-Moûtier, Châlons, Isabelle Romée, La Trémoille. This differs from the Alexander story, whose English names carry
  no diacritics; its rule is for Greek names put into Latin letters, and these are names written in French already.
- Old French spellings of the sources (Jehanne, Sainct, Orléens, Rains, Baugency) and the OCR's broken forms are
  kept only inside a quote, copied as the file has them (`rules.md`).
- Terms: the Dauphin (a title, with a capital); men-at-arms; lances, as the sources count them; archers; bastille;
  boulevard; the Holy Ampulla; the trial of 1431 (the trial of condemnation) and the retrial of 1455–1456 (the
  trial of nullification), "the retrial" in a text; abjuration and relapse; bailli; livres tournois.
