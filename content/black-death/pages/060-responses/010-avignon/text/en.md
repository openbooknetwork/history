The popes have lived at Avignon since 1309, and Clement VI is building a new palace there. The plague comes up the Rhône from [Marseille](@marseille) and reaches Avignon in January 1348. Guy de Chauliac, the pope’s doctor, says it lasts seven months. On 27 April Louis Heyligen, a Flemish musician in a cardinal’s household, writes from Avignon that half the city or more has died.

The cemeteries fill. The pope buys a field outside the walls at Champfleury and has it consecrated, and Heyligen writes that 11,000 bodies are buried there in six weeks. Clement also consecrates the Rhône, so that the dead can be laid in the river. In mid-March, Heyligen reports, he pardons until Easter the sins of all who die of the plague after confessing and repenting.

Doctors hold that fire cleans the corrupted air. Clement stays shut in a room of the [Palace of the Popes](@papal-palace) with great fires always burning, the chronicler Mathias of Neuenburg writes, and sees no one. By one account the fires are Guy’s advice. The pope lives until 1352. Guy, by his own account, catches the plague and recovers. In his book on surgery he tells two forms apart: the sick who cough blood die within three days, those with swellings in the armpits or the groin within five.

When the killings of Jews begin in Provence, at Toulon in April, Clement and the city’s councillors prevent a massacre of the Jews of Avignon. It is said that he takes the Jews of his county of the Venaissin under his protection as well. His bulls of July and September 1348 forbid Christians to kill Jews without trial or to rob them, and by one account to baptise them by force. In the second, of 26 September, he writes that those who blame the Jews have been “seduced by that liar, the Devil”, because the plague kills Jews too, and kills where no Jews have ever lived. The bulls do not stop the burnings in the Empire in 1349.
