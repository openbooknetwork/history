In the Late Postclassic, trading canoes follow the coast of the Yucatán Peninsula from Honduras to Tabasco and Campeche. Xicalango, in the land of the Chontal Maya, draws merchants from all of Mesoamerica. The province of Chetumal, the one large cacao grower in Yucatán, holds a quarter of the port of Nito on the Dulce River. Yucatán sends salt, cotton cloth and honey, and receives cacao, obsidian, jade and copper. The Motagua and the Usumacinta carry goods on into the highlands.

Tulum, perhaps called Zama, stands on a cliff above the Caribbean from about 1200. A wall 3 to 5 m high, 400 m long and pierced by five narrow gates, closes it on the land side. A shrine on the Castillo marks a gap in the reef, and canoes land on a small beach below. Cozumel, a port and place of pilgrimage from about 1250, draws merchants and pilgrims to the shrine of the goddess Ixchel.

On 30 July 1502, on his fourth voyage, Christopher Columbus reaches Guanaja in the Bay Islands. A canoe cut from one tree trunk, 2.5 m wide and rowed by 25 men, comes from the west. His brother Bartholomew boards it. It carries cacao, cotton cloth, pottery, copper axes and bells, and wooden swords edged with flint. The Spaniards take what they want and keep the old captain as an interpreter. By the usual account the canoe comes from Yucatán, and this is the first recorded meeting of Europeans and Maya. Some scholars hold that its crew came from the Honduran coast.
