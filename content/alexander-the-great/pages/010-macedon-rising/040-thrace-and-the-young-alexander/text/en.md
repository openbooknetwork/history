In summer 342 BC Philip, already master of western Thrace, marches inland against the Odrysian kings of the Hebrus valley. In two years of fighting that the sources barely record, he deposes them, and Thrace becomes a subject land under a Macedonian general, paying a tenth of its produce in tribute. Philip plants garrisons and settlers in new towns between the Haemus mountains and the Hebrus, among them [Philippopolis](@philippopolis-342), today Plovdiv. No source gives a northern border; the line drawn along the Haemus, like the marches, is approximate.

At home Alexander is fourteen, and Philip invites the philosopher Aristotle to teach him. Plutarch places their lessons in the precinct of the Nymphs near [Mieza](@mieza-342), and tells, without a date, how the boy tamed Bucephalus, a horse that Philoneicus the Thessalian offered Philip for thirteen talents: seeing that it shied at its own shadow, he turned it to face the sun and mounted it.

In spring 340 BC Philip lays siege to [Perinthus](@perinthus-340), a Greek city on the Propontis friendly to Athens. His rams breach the wall, but the houses behind it, Diodorus writes, rise in rows up a steep hill, and each row serves as a new wall. [Byzantium](@byzantium-340) sends men and catapults, and the Great King of Persia, alarmed, has his satraps send mercenaries, money and food. In the autumn Philip turns on Byzantium itself and seizes an Athenian grain fleet, and Athens declares war. With Athenian, Chian and Rhodian ships defending the city, he gives up early in 339.

Meanwhile Alexander, sixteen, rules Macedon from [Pella](@pella-359) as regent, holding the royal seal, Plutarch says. When the [Maedi](@maedi-340) of the upper Strymon revolt, he marches up the Axius and across to them, takes their town, resettles it and names it Alexandropolis. No source this book follows places it.
