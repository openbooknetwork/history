By the end of 1353 the plague has killed an estimated 24 to 46 million people in the lands where a count can be made. How far it reached between the towns with a known date is an estimate: each such town is given the land nearer to it than to any other. Historians rebuild the toll from records kept for other purposes.

Among these records are the taxpayers of [Asyut](@asyut), the bishops’ registers of [England](@dead-england), and the book of citizens of [Bremen](@bremen-1350) with its 6,966 dead. At [Givry](@givry-1348) in Burgundy the parish buries perhaps as many people in under four months of 1348 as in the twenty years before, 615 by Pierre Gras’s count of its register. In [Florence](@florence-1348) Boccaccio counts more than 100,000 dead, close to the whole city of about 120,000.

Where few records survive, as around [Prague](@prague) and in [Poland](@dead-poland), the toll is hard to measure, and the estimate’s range there is wide.

The estimate multiplies the people of each region about 1346 by the share that dies, low and high. Most regions of Europe take a third as the low share, the estimate of older histories, and the 60 percent of the historian Ole Benedictow as the high. [Egypt](@dead-egypt) takes a third to half, as the historian Michael Dols estimates. The rates of [Syria](@dead-syria), a quarter to 38 percent, and of the [Maghreb](@dead-maghreb), a quarter to a third, are assumptions, set around Dols’s third for the Middle East. Central Asia, the Golden Horde, Iraq and Arabia stay out of the sum, because no figure for their people survives.

For Europe alone, historians give 25 to 50 million dead, a third to 60 percent of the 80 million people Benedictow counts. The regions of the estimate hold about 72 million Europeans, so its share for Europe is lower.
