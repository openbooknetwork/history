The plague reaches Florence from [Pisa](@pisa) and the coast in March 1348 and lasts until July. Florence is one of the largest cities of Europe. In the 1330s the chronicler [Giovanni Villani](@villani) put its people at about 90,000, reckoning from the bread that the city ate each day.

@image florence-bigallo-1342-fe0a80

Giovanni Boccaccio lives through the plague in Florence and describes it at the opening of the Decameron, which he writes in the years after. The sick break out in swellings in the groin or the armpit, some as large as an apple, then in black or purple spots, and most of them die within three days. Fear breaks the bonds of families, he writes, so that “fathers and mothers were found to abandon their own children, untended, unvisited, to their fate, as if they had been strangers.” He puts the dead at more than 100,000 between March and July, more than the city holds.

The chronicler Marchionne di Coppo Stefani tells how every church digs deep trenches for the dead. Each night the bodies of the day are laid in and covered with a little earth, and the next bodies are laid on top of them, layer upon layer, in the way that cheese is layered in lasagne.

Villani keeps his chronicle of the city to the end. His last chapter tells of the plague and breaks off in the middle of a sentence: “And this plague lasted until …” He dies of it in 1348 with the date unwritten, and his brother Matteo carries the chronicle on.
