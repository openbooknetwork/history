At the end of spring 327 BC Alexander leads the army out of [Bactra](@bactra-327b) towards India. He crosses the [Hindu Kush](@hindu-kush-327) in ten days and marches down the valley of the [Cophen](@cophen-327), today the Kabul River. By Alexander's time Persian rule, even in name, reached no further than this valley. Bosworth thinks that spurred Alexander to rebuild and widen the empire he had won, and that he may already have thought of carrying his conquests on to the Ocean.

In Arrian's account Taxiles, ruler of Taxila beyond the Indus, and the other chiefs west of the river come to meet him with gifts. Here Alexander divides the army. Hephaestion, his closest friend, and Perdiccas, one of his senior generals, take about half the Macedonians and all the mercenaries down the main road along the river. They are to win over or storm the towns on the way and [bridge the Indus](@indus-bridge-327).

Alexander takes his best troops north of the river into the hills of [Bajaur](@bajaur-327) and [Swat](@swat-327). In Bosworth's reading he expects the hill peoples to submit at once: a town that resists is stormed and its defenders killed, and the next one surrenders.

The largest town of the region, [Massaga](@massaga-327), holds out with the help of 7,000 mercenaries from further inside India. Its site is unknown; Bosworth places it north of the Swat river, near the Katgala pass. An arrow from the wall wounds Alexander in the ankle. After four days of assaults the town's commander is killed and the defenders ask for terms. The mercenaries are to join Alexander's army, and they march out with their arms and camp on a hill. There they are cut down.\*

### Notes

\* Arrian says the mercenaries planned to slip home by night rather than fight other Indians, so Alexander surrounded the hill in the dark and killed them. Diodorus has them leave under the truce, suspecting nothing, until Alexander follows them and falls on them. Plutarch calls the deed a stain on Alexander's record as a soldier.
