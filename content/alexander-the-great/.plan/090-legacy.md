# Legacy

## Header
- Years: June 323 BC to 301 BC, and after
- Opens: Alexander has died in Babylon between the evenings of 10 and 11 June 323 BC (080.12), and the sources do not
  know the cause (080.13). His empire runs from Macedon and Thrace to Egypt, Sogdiana and the Indus delta
  (macedon-325). Antipater governs Macedon and Greece as general of Europe. Craterus is on his way home with the
  10,000 veterans (080.8). Roxana is pregnant and has had Stateira killed (080.14). The army has laid aside the last
  plans (080.10). The Greek states are in the League of Corinth, Sparta outside it. Taxiles and Porus hold their
  kingdoms on the Indus and the Hydaspes.
- Tells:
  - June 323 BC: the Babylon settlement. Arrhidaeus becomes king as Philip III, with a share kept for Roxana's child;
    Perdiccas becomes regent; the generals take the satrapies (090.1, 090.2)
  - 323–322 BC: the Lamian War. Athens and the Aetolians rise, Leosthenes shuts Antipater up in Lamia and dies;
    Craterus joins Antipater; Crannon, 322 BC; Antipater's terms for Athens (090.3–090.5, 090.30)
  - 321 BC (for Bosworth too, Legacy appendix; Triparadisus autumn 321 for H&W, spring 320 for Bosworth): Perdiccas invades Egypt, fails at the Nile and is killed by his officers; Craterus has fallen against
    Eumenes; the settlement of Triparadisus: Antipater guardian of the kings, Seleucus satrap of Babylonia,
    Antigonus general against Eumenes (090.6, 090.7)
  - 320–319 BC: Nicanor, Ptolemy's general, takes Laomedon captive, and Ptolemy holds Syria and Phoenicia (090.27)
  - September 317 BC: Olympias has Philip III murdered (090.28). Winter 317–316 BC: Cassander shuts Olympias, Roxana
    and Alexander IV in Pydna; in spring 316 BC Olympias capitulates and is put to death, and Cassander holds Macedon
    (090.29)
  - Winter 317–316 BC: Gabiene; the Silver Shields hand Eumenes over (090.8, 090.31). 316–315 BC: Antigonus, master
    of Asia, is treated as king in Persis; Seleucus flees Babylon to Ptolemy (090.9)
  - 315–314 BC: Antigonus takes Syria and Phoenicia from Ptolemy (090.33). 312 BC: Gaza; Seleucus regains
    Babylonia (090.10). 311 BC: the peace; Cassander general of Europe; Alexander IV
    and Roxana murdered on Cassander's order (090.11, 090.21). By 306–305 BC Seleucus holds the upper satrapies
    and cedes the eastern satrapies to Chandragupta, date unknown (090.22, 090.23). 306 BC: Salamis; Antigonus and
    Demetrius take the title of king; Ptolemy (305 BC) and the others follow (090.12)
  - 302 BC: the allies' marches on Asia Minor (090.32). 301 BC: Ipsus; Antigonus is killed; the victors carve up his
    kingdom (090.13–090.15, 090.24–090.26)
  - After 301 BC: the Alexandrias, the koine, the Alexander Romance (090.16, 090.18, 090.20)
- Introduces: people: Philip III (Arrhidaeus), Alexander IV, Laomedon, Philotas, satrap of Cilicia, Peithon,
  Eumenes, Lysimachus, Leosthenes, Antiphilus, Menon the Thessalian, Hypereides, Neoptolemus,
  Seleucus, Nicanor, Ptolemy's general, Demetrius, son of Antigonus, Glaucias, Cassander's officer,
  Antiochus, Chandragupta, Pleistarchus (named once as disputed); Cassander and
  Antigonus take their main part here; Demosthenes, introduced earlier, gets a clause. States: the Successors (Diadochi), the Greeks of the Lamian War, the
  Aetolians, the Silver Shields, the kingdom of Antigonus, Ptolemaic Egypt, the Seleucid Empire, the kingdom of
  Lysimachus, the Mauryan Empire. Places: Lamia, Crannon, Munychia, the Nile, Triparadisus (site unknown, named
  only), Pydna (named only), Paraetacene and Gabiene, Carrhae, Salamis in Cyprus, Antigonia on the Orontes, Ipsus,
  Coele-Syria, Alexandria in Areia, Alexandria in Arachosia, Alexandria Troas. Thermopylae, Pelusium, Sidon and
  Alexandria in the Caucasus, introduced in earlier sections, get a clause each where they come back.
- Battles: Crannon (card, no phase pages), Gabiene (card, no phase pages), Ipsus (card, no phase pages). Gaza 312
  BC is told without a card.
- Hands on: the story ends here. After Ipsus, Cassander keeps Macedon; Lysimachus holds Thrace and Asia Minor north
  of the Taurus; Seleucus holds Syria, Mesopotamia and the east to the Hindu Kush and the Jaxartes, the lands on
  the Indus having gone to Chandragupta; Ptolemy holds Egypt and Coele-Syria. Left unsettled: Seleucus's claim to
  Coele-Syria, which Ptolemy refuses (090.15, 090.25); Demetrius, still holding Tyre and Sidon (Tarn) and the sea;
  Pleistarchus's share, which the sources place in two lands (090.26).

## Pages

**010-babylon-settlement**, June 323 BC. Alexander dies with no grown heir, and the cavalry and infantry clash over
the succession. The army makes Arrhidaeus king as Philip III and keeps a share for Roxana's child if it is a son;
Perdiccas, who holds the ring, becomes regent; the generals share the satrapies: Ptolemy Egypt, Laomedon Syria,
Antigonus Pamphylia, Lycia and Greater Phrygia, Eumenes Cappadocia and Paphlagonia, Leonnatus Hellespontine
Phrygia, Lysimachus Thrace, Antipater Macedon; the eastern satraps, Porus and Taxiles keep their lands. Why: no heir
of age, an army split between a half-brother of the king and an unborn child, and generals who each wanted a share.
Map: the empire becomes the empire of the kings, cut by the satrapies of Ptolemy, Antigonus, Eumenes and
Lysimachus.
Rests on: 090.1, 090.2; 080.12 for the date of the death, recalled in a clause.

**020-lamian-war**, 323–322 BC. On the news of the death Athens and the Aetolians rise with most of central Greece,
Thessaly and part of the Peloponnese; the Boeotians stay with Macedon. Leosthenes holds Thermopylae, beats
Antipater and shuts him up in Lamia for the winter; Leosthenes is killed at the siege. Craterus comes from Asia and
joins Antipater in Thessaly; at Crannon in 322 BC the Greek cavalry has the better of it, the infantry gives way,
and the battle is broken off in the Macedonians' favour; Antipater then makes peace city by city. Athens surrenders
Demosthenes and Hypereides, takes a property franchise, a garrison in Munychia and an indemnity, and more than
12,000 poorer citizens lose their rights. Why: the Athenians claim the leadership of the Greeks once Alexander is
dead (Diodorus 18.10). Map: Greece in arms against Macedon; Antipater's march to Lamia and on to Crannon.
Rests on: 090.3, 090.4, 090.5, 090.30. 090.4 and 090.30 are single: the figures on the card are Diodorus's and
the text names him.

**030-triparadisus**, 321–320 BC. Perdiccas, wary of Ptolemy's power, takes the kings and the royal army against Egypt
and sends Eumenes to hold the Hellespont against Antipater and Craterus. Eumenes beats them in Asia Minor, and
Craterus and Neoptolemus fall. At the Nile Perdiccas fails near Pelusium and again opposite Memphis, and his
officers, Peithon among them, stab him in his tent. At Triparadisus in Syria, a site that is not known, the army
makes Antipater guardian of the kings; Ptolemy keeps Egypt, Seleucus gets Babylonia, Antigonus keeps Phrygia and
Lycia and is made general of the royal army against Eumenes. Why: the regent's war against a satrap who would not
be displaced. Map: Perdiccas's march to Memphis; Babylonia passes to Seleucus.
Rests on: 090.6, 090.7. Both single (Diodorus; Justin agrees in outline for 090.6): the text tells them through
Diodorus where it gives a detail. The invasion is 321 BC for Hammond and Walbank, Geer and Bosworth (Legacy,
appendix); only the date of Triparadisus differs, autumn 321 or spring 320 BC, and goes into a note.

**040-gabiene**, 317–315 BC. Since 320–319 BC Ptolemy has held Syria and Phoenicia, his general Nicanor having
taken Laomedon captive (090.27). In Macedon Olympias returns and in September 317 BC has Philip III stabbed to death
(090.28); Cassander comes back from the Peloponnese, shuts Olympias, Roxana and Alexander IV in Pydna for the winter,
and in spring 316 BC Olympias capitulates and is put to death, leaving Cassander master of Macedon (090.29).
Antigonus, sent against Eumenes, follows him into Iran. Eumenes, with the satraps of
the east and the Silver Shields, marches from Babylonia by Susa to Persepolis, and the armies meet at Paraetacene
and, in winter 317–316 BC, at Gabiene. There Antigonus's horse takes Eumenes's baggage behind the dust of the
battle, and the Silver Shields, victorious in their part of the field, hand Eumenes over to get their families back;
Antigonus has him put to death. Antigonus, master of Asia, is treated as king in Persis and redistributes the eastern
satrapies; Seleucus flees Babylon to Ptolemy. Why: the war the settlement of Triparadisus gave Antigonus, and the
army's families in the baggage. Map: the two marches into Iran; Antigonus's Asia from the Aegean to the Hindu Kush,
Ptolemy holding Syria and Phoenicia, Macedon under Cassander.
Rests on: 090.27, 090.28, 090.29, 090.8, 090.31, 090.9. 090.8, 090.9, 090.27 and 090.31 are single (Diodorus;
Plutarch's Eumenes goes back to the same Hieronymus): the figures on the card name Diodorus. 090.29 is agreed for
the siege of Pydna and single for Olympias's surrender and death, which the text tells as Hammond and Walbank's.
The site of Gabiene is not known and the text says so.

**050-the-kings**, 312–305 BC. A clause opens the page: in 315–314 BC Antigonus took Syria and Phoenicia from
Ptolemy (090.33), so the coast between Egypt and Asia Minor is his when the page begins. At Gaza in 312 BC Ptolemy and Seleucus beat Demetrius, son of Antigonus; Seleucus
goes back with a small force through Mesopotamia and regains Babylonia. The peace of 311 BC gives Cassander Europe
as general until Alexander IV comes of age, Lysimachus Thrace, Ptolemy Egypt with the cities next to it in Libya and
Arabia, and Antigonus the first place in all Asia; soon after, Cassander has the boy and Roxana murdered by Glaucias.
Seleucus goes east to the confines of India and by 306–305 BC holds the upper satrapies of Iran besides Babylonia;
in a peace with Chandragupta he cedes the eastern satrapies for 500 elephants, at a date no source read gives.
Antigonus founds Antigonia on the Orontes to watch Babylon and the upper satrapies. After Demetrius's victory at
Salamis in Cyprus in 306 BC, Antigonus takes the diadem and the title of king and gives it to Demetrius; Ptolemy
follows in 305 BC and the others too. Why: no heir is left, so each holds his land "as if it were a kingdom won by
the spear" (Diodorus 19.105.4). Map: Seleucus's march back to Babylon and Demetrius's voyage to Salamis; the kingdom
of Antigonus and Seleucus's lands from Babylonia to the Hindu Kush.
Rests on: 090.33, 090.10,
090.11, 090.21, 090.22, 090.12, 090.23. 090.23: the cession is told in Bosworth's words, "the
eastern satrapies"; the lands Hammond and Walbank name (Gandhara, eastern Arachosia and Gedrosia) are told as theirs
alone, with no date, and no line is drawn for them.

**060-ipsus**, 302–301 BC. Cassander, Lysimachus, Seleucus and Ptolemy join against Antigonus. Cassander holds the
Thessalian passes against Demetrius; Lysimachus, reinforced by Cassander, crosses into Asia in early summer 302 BC
and evades Antigonus until winter; Seleucus comes from the upper satrapies into Cappadocia with his elephants;
Antigonus comes up from Syria and calls Demetrius over from Greece in the autumn; Ptolemy invades Syria and draws
back on a false report, yet holds Coele-Syria, and does not fight at Ipsus. At Ipsus in Phrygia
in 301 BC Demetrius's cavalry routs Antiochus but pursues too far; Seleucus's elephants cut him off, the exposed
phalanx deserts, and Antigonus falls under many missiles. The victors carve up his kingdom: Lysimachus
takes Asia Minor north of the Taurus, Cappadocia included, though Pontus and Bithynia are independent and many Greek
cities hold to Demetrius; Seleucus takes Syria and Mesopotamia, his empire stretching in theory from the Hindu Kush
and the Jaxartes to the Mediterranean, though Tyre and Sidon stay with Demetrius; Ptolemy keeps Coele-Syria and
refuses Seleucus's claim to it; Cassander keeps Macedon. Why: the others would not let one man take Alexander's whole
empire. Map: the four armies converge on Phrygia; the kingdom of Antigonus is divided.
Rests on: 090.32, 090.13, 090.14, 090.15, 090.24, 090.25, 090.26. 090.32 is agreed (Hammond and Walbank; Diodorus
20.107–113); Seleucus's 480 elephants on the march and the allies' 400 at Ipsus (090.14) are each told with their
author. 090.13 is agreed for the outcome and single for the course
(Plutarch, whom Hammond and Walbank follow): the text names Plutarch for the course. 090.14 single: the strengths
name Plutarch. 090.25 disputed for how far north Ptolemy held: the text gives Tarn (Syria south of Damascus and the
Lebanon) and Bosworth (as far as Sidon, winter 302–301 BC). 090.26 disputed for Pleistarchus: one clause says
Cassander's brother got a small share south of the Taurus, which Bosworth places in Cilicia and Tarn in Caria, and
the map leaves it off.

**070-alexandrias**, after 301 BC. Alexander's foundations and what outlived him: Alexandria in Egypt, laid out as a
Greek city; the garrison cities of the east, Alexandria in Areia, Alexandria in Arachosia and Alexandria in the Caucasus, and Alexandria
Eschate on the Jaxartes; Bucephala and Nicaea on the Hydaspes, named only, since their sites are not known.
Plutarch credits him with seventy cities among barbarian peoples; Bosworth calls this divorced from reality, the
foundations being garrison points of conquest, and several Alexandrias of Asia Minor and the Near East likely
foundations of the Successors (Alexandria Troas, refounded by Lysimachus). The Greek koine becomes almost a
universal language of culture, though no political or economic union followed (the Cambridge Ancient History). The
Alexander Romance, a mostly fantastical life around a historical core, carries stories of his death that go back to
the propaganda of the Successors. Why: the cities held a conquered land, and the Successors' kingdoms carried Greek
on. Map: the Hellenistic kingdoms of 301 BC with the Alexandrias marked.
Rests on: 090.16, 090.18, 090.20. 090.16 is disputed (told in a note): Plutarch's seventy cities and Bosworth's
reading, each with its author. 090.18 is single and names the Cambridge Ancient History. Ai-Khanoum (090.17) and
Gandhara art (090.19) are not told; Gandhara may be named as a place only.

## Battles

**crannon-322**, Crannon in Thessaly, 322 BC (probably September, Geer's note after Dinsmoor, in the file of 090.4).
- Sides: the Greeks of the Lamian War (`greeks`) against the Macedonians (`macedon`).
- Commanders: Antiphilus and Menon the Thessalian (Diodorus 18.17.6, 090.30); Antipater and Craterus, Antipater in
  chief command (Diodorus 18.16.5).
- Strengths (090.30): Greeks 25,000 foot and 3,500 horse (Diodorus 18.17.2); Macedonians more than 40,000 heavy infantry,
  3,000 bowmen and slingers and 5,000 horse (Diodorus 18.16.5).
- Casualties: Greeks more than 500 (Diodorus 18.17.5); Macedonians 130 (Diodorus 18.17.5).
- Outcome: the Greek cavalry wins its fight, the Greek infantry draws back to the high ground, and the battle is
  broken off in the Macedonians' favour; the Greeks sue for peace and Antipater treats with the cities one by one.
- File: penelope.uchicago.edu-Thayer-E-Roman-Texts-Diodorus-Siculus-18A.txt, 18.16–18.17. No phase pages.

**gabiene-316**, Gabiene, winter 317–316 BC; the site is not known.
- Sides: Antigonus (`antigonus`) against Eumenes (`eumenes`).
- Commanders: Antigonus, Demetrius and Peithon (Diodorus 19.40.1); Eumenes, Antigenes of the Silver Shields and
  Peucestas (Diodorus 19.40–19.42).
- Strengths (090.31): Antigonus 22,000 foot, 9,000 horse and 65 elephants (Diodorus 19.40.1); Eumenes 36,700 foot, 6,000
  horse and 114 elephants (Diodorus 19.40.4).
- Casualties: Antigonus's infantry lost more than 5,000 to the Silver Shields, who lost none (Diodorus 19.43.1);
  Eumenes's other losses `unknown`, his baggage taken (090.31).
- Outcome: Antigonus's horse takes the baggage behind the dust, and the Silver Shields hand Eumenes over to recover
  their families; Antigonus has him put to death (Diodorus 19.43.8, 19.44.2).
- File: penelope.uchicago.edu-Thayer-E-Roman-Texts-Diodorus-Siculus-19B.txt, 19.39–19.44. Diodorus gives both
  sides' order of battle (19.40), but the battle keeps its card only, by the cloud ruling and outline.md (it did not
  decide a war of the story's subject). No phase pages.

**ipsus-301**, Ipsus in Phrygia, 301 BC.
- Sides: Antigonus (`antigonus`) against Seleucus (`seleucid`) and Lysimachus (`lysimachus`). The allies' strength is
  given for both together, so the card puts them in one column, "Seleucus and Lysimachus", coloured `seleucid`.
- Commanders: Antigonus (killed) and Demetrius; Seleucus, Lysimachus and Antiochus, son of Seleucus (Plutarch,
  Demetrius 28–29). Cassander's contingent and Pleistarchus are not on the card.
- Strengths: Antigonus more than 70,000 foot, 10,000 horse and 75 elephants; the allies 64,000 foot, 10,500 horse,
  400 elephants and 120 chariots (Plutarch, Demetrius 28; Hammond and Walbank repeat him, p. 178).
- Casualties: `unknown` on both sides. Antigonus is killed (Diodorus 21.1.4b; Plutarch, Demetrius 29).
- Outcome: victory of the allies; Antigonus killed, Demetrius flees, the kingdom of Antigonus is partitioned.
- Files: penelope.uchicago.edu-Thayer-E-Roman-Texts-Plutarch-Lives-Demetrius.txt, chs. 28–30; the H&W file, pp.
  178–179. No phase pages.

Gaza 312 BC (090.10) is told on 050-the-kings with a marker and no card.

## Choices
- Seven pages where the brief expected six. The brief's line "the wars of the Successors" holds three changes of the
  map in different lands years apart: Babylonia to Seleucus at Triparadisus (321–320 BC, Egypt and Syria), Antigonus
  master of Asia (316 BC, Iran), and the kingdoms of 306 BC. Each takes a page (030, 040, 050). Two pages are
  saved elsewhere: Gaza 312 and the kings of 306–305 share 050, and the legend shares 070 with the Alexandrias,
  since the Romance changes no map.
- 050 merges 312–305 BC into one page: Seleucus's return to Babylon (312) and his kingdom of 306–305 are one change
  in one land within a few years; the map shows the end state, 306 BC.
- The Triparadisus page takes Perdiccas's war and Craterus's death with it: they are the cause of the settlement.
- macedon-322 (now in the Map part) is a `zones.yaml` entry on the outline of macedon-348 for the Lamian War page, so Thessaly can
  show among the Greeks.
- ptolemaic-319 shows Ptolemy's hold on Syria and Phoenicia in 316 BC (090.27); the Map part now carries it, and
  ptolemaic-323 stands for 320 BC and for 315 BC to Ipsus. Antigonus's taking of Syria and Phoenicia in 315–314 BC is
  told in a clause at the start of 050 (090.33), where ptolemaic-323 and antigonus-306 take over; 040's map stays
  at 316 BC, with Syria still Ptolemy's.
- The eastern edge of antigonus-316, seleucid-306 and seleucid-301 follows persia-336's: Tarn's "Hindu Kush and the
  Jaxartes" (090.25) and Bosworth's note that India west of the Indus had no Macedonian satrap after 316 (090.23).
  This draws no line for the cession, as the cloud ruling asks.
- lysimachus-301 covers Pontus and Bithynia as the partition's terms, "all Asia Minor north of the Taurus" (Tarn,
  Bosworth); no source gives the bounds of the two kingdoms, so cutting them out would draw a line nothing leads to.
  The text says they were independent.
- The south of Asia Minor (Lycia, Pamphylia, Cilicia, Caria) is left without a zone on 060 and 070: Pleistarchus's
  share is left off the map by the cloud ruling, and no source gives these lands to anyone else.
- Pleistarchus is named once on 060, as disputed, so the blank south coast has its reason.
- On the Ipsus card Seleucus and Lysimachus share one column, since Plutarch gives their strength together.
- Gabiene keeps its card only, though Diodorus gives both orders of battle (cloud ruling; outline.md).
- Tyre and Sidon are uncoloured markers on 060, since Tarn and Bosworth differ on who held the coast as far as
  Sidon.
- Greece south of Thessaly has no zone from 030 on: no source in the digest says who held it after the Lamian War.
- 040 tells Philip III's murder, Pydna and Cassander's hold (090.28, 090.29) in its text without a marker or route,
  since its map is Iran; they fall in the same years and give the page its Macedon zone.
- The marker ids take the year (babylon-323b, babylon-315, babylon-312) so they do not clash with the markers of
  other sections.
