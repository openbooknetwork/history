Henry V comes back in 1417. He lands at Touques in Normandy on 1 August, as Ramsay dates it, and marches along the coast to [Caen](@caen-1417), the first town of his conquest, which falls to an assault on 4 September. He takes Normandy town by town and spares the countryside. He comes before [Rouen](@rouen-1419) on 29 July 1418, and the starving city surrenders on 19 January 1419. By the end of 1419 only [Mont-Saint-Michel](@mont-saint-michel-1419) is left to the French.

Meanwhile, in May and June 1418, the Burgundians take [Paris](@paris-1418) and massacre the Armagnacs, the Count of Armagnac among them. The king's last surviving son, the Dauphin Charles, fifteen years old by Lowell's reckoning and sixteen by Lang's, is carried out of the city and flees to [Bourges](@bourges-1418).

On 10 September 1419 John the Fearless meets the Dauphin to make peace, inside a fenced enclosure on the bridge of [Montereau](@montereau-1419), where the Yonne flows into the Seine. There the duke is killed, his head split with an axe. Just how it happened, and what the Dauphin knew, is disputed.\* His son, Philip the Good, takes the Burgundians over to Henry V.

By a treaty sealed in the cathedral of [Troyes](@troyes-1420) on 21 May 1420, Charles VI makes Henry regent of France and heir to his crown, so that at his death the two kingdoms will share one king. The Dauphin is disinherited and branded with the murder of Montereau, and no one is to treat with him. On 2 June, Ramsay's day, Henry marries the king's daughter, Catherine of Valois, in the same cathedral.

The map now shows Normandy, Paris, the Île-de-France and Champagne as English and Picardy as the Duke of Burgundy's, though the Dauphin's men still hold [Melun](@melun-1420), [Meaux](@meaux-1420), [Soissons](@soissons-1420) and [Compiègne](@compiegne-1420) inside that land. The Dauphin's party keeps the rest of the kingdom. The zones are approximate, and so is the line of Henry's march to Caen. On the Meuse, Lowell writes, the Burgundians invade Bar, and the Duke of Lorraine swears allegiance to Henry V.

### Notes

\* In the Burgundian account, which Ramsay follows after Monstrelet, armed men rushed on the duke from behind the Dauphin as he knelt before him. In the Armagnac account that Wylie and Waugh report, he was struck only after he had offered violence to the Dauphin. Lang writes that whether the Dauphin knew of the plan beforehand, and whether he was there when it was carried out, are both in dispute.
