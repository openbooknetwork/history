On Wednesday 15 June the army leaves [Orléans](@orleans-15-june-1429) and storms the fortified bridge of [Meung](@meung-bridge-1429), leaving the town and castle to the English. On Thursday 16 June it comes before [Beaugency](@beaugency-1429). Matthew Gough, a Welsh captain and Lord Talbot's lieutenant, and Sir Richard Gethin, bailli of Évreux, have left the town for its castle and bridge. Perceval de Cagny, a chronicler of the Duke of Alençon's household, gives them 400 English, and Lowell fewer than a thousand men. Men hidden in the houses fall on the French as they settle in.

On Friday 17 June Arthur de Richemont, Constable of France, comes with 500 or 600 fighting men, by Cagny's count.\* Charles VII, urged by his favourite Georges de La Trémoille, had forbidden it, and Alençon has orders not to receive him. How Joan greets him is told two ways.\*\*

Meanwhile an English relief from [Paris](@paris-fastolf-1429) under Sir John Fastolf has halted at [Janville](@janville-1429) on the news that Jargeau has fallen, and Talbot rides in from Beaugency to join it. Jean de Wavrin, a chronicler who was there, says that Fastolf advised going no further, and that Talbot swore to fight with his own men alone if he had to.

On 17 June the English come within about a league of Meung and Beaugency and find the French drawn up on a small hill. They dismount, and two heralds offer three knights to fight anyone who comes down. It is late, the French reply in Wavrin's telling: find quarters for tonight, and tomorrow we shall see you nearer. The English turn back, take the town of Meung and pound its bridge all night.

In that night Gough agrees terms. On the morning of Saturday 18 June his men ride off through the Beauce with their lives, horses and harness, and the French enter.\*\*\*

The roads on the map are approximate, and the hill, which no source places, is not marked.

### Notes

\* Lang, following Gruel, Richemont's biographer, has him come on 16 June. At Selles on 8 June Guy de Laval had heard that he was coming with 600 men-at-arms and 400 archers.

\*\* In Gruel she kneels to him, and he answers that he does not know whether she comes from God or from the devil. In the Chronique de la Pucelle he begs her pardon in the king's name. Lowell holds both accounts fantastical.

\*\*\* The Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of Orléans, has them swear not to bear arms for ten days. Lang, reading Gruel, thinks the surrender came a night earlier, at midnight on 16 to 17 June, though he allows that it may have been 17 to 18 June.
