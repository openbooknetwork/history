The regent Perdiccas goes to war with Ptolemy in 321 BC. Ptolemy has made Egypt strong with mercenaries and the treasury he found there, and he has taken Alexander's body into Egypt.\* Antipater and Craterus, who believe that Perdiccas means to invade Macedon and remove them, cross into Asia. Perdiccas takes the kings and the royal army against Egypt and sends Eumenes to hold the Hellespont against the two generals. Eumenes beats Neoptolemus, a general who had gone over to Antipater, and then Craterus himself, and both fall.

Perdiccas reaches the Nile near [Pelusium](@pelusium-320). He fails to take a fort there, then tries to cross to an island opposite [Memphis](@memphis-320), but the ford deepens under the feet of the men and animals, and the crossing fails. Diodorus says more than 2,000 men are lost, drowned or eaten by crocodiles. About a hundred officers break with Perdiccas, Peithon, satrap of Media, the most famous of them, and some of the cavalry stab him to death in his tent. News of Eumenes's victory arrives soon after, and the army condemns Eumenes to death.

At Triparadisus\*\*, a royal park in Syria whose site is not known for certain, the army makes Antipater guardian of the kings, and he shares out the satrapies again. Ptolemy keeps Egypt, which he holds, Diodorus writes, like a prize won in war, and Cyrene, which his general Ophellas took in 322 BC. Seleucus, who had commanded the Companion cavalry, receives Babylonia. Antigonus keeps Greater Phrygia and Lycia and becomes general of the royal army, with orders to finish the war against Eumenes, who still holds Cappadocia.

Perdiccas's march up the Nile ends opposite Memphis, and Babylonia passes to Seleucus.

### Notes

\* Hammond and Walbank say the funeral carriage was bound for Aegae in Macedon and was diverted at Damascus. Diodorus says the body was meant for the shrine of Ammon, and that Ptolemy met it in Syria and kept it in Egypt.

\*\* Hammond and Walbank date the meeting at Triparadisus to the autumn of 321 BC; Bosworth dates it to the spring of 320 BC.
