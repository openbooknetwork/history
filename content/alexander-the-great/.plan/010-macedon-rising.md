# Macedon rising

## Header
- Years: 360 BC (late 360 or 359: Perdiccas III falls; Philip II takes the kingdom; Ellis ch. 14 §III, Diodorus
  16.2.1) to October 336 BC (Philip killed at Aegae;
  Alexander acclaimed king)
- Opens: Macedon is a kingdom between the Illyrians in the west and the Thracians east of the Strymon. Its king,
  Perdiccas III, has just been killed with more than 4,000 of his men by the Illyrians, and the Illyrians hold valleys
  of Upper Macedon. The Chalcidian League under Olynthus holds the Chalcidice; Amphipolis is independent. The
  reader has been told only the story's summary: who Alexander was and where the book ends.
- Tells:
  - late 360 or 359 BC: Perdiccas III killed by the Illyrians; Philip takes the kingdom (010.1, 010.2)
  - 360–358 BC: Philip drills a new army: the phalanx, the sarissa, the Companion cavalry (010.3, 010.26)
  - 358 BC: Philip beats Bardylis's Illyrians and takes Upper Macedon to Lake Lychnitis (010.4, 010.23, 010.24)
  - 357 BC: Amphipolis taken by siege (010.25)
  - 356 BC: Crenides refounded as Philippi; the gold of Mount Pangaeum (010.5, 010.6)
  - July 356 BC: Alexander born to Philip and Olympias (010.10)
  - early summer 352 BC: the Crocus Field; Thessaly comes under Philip (010.7)
  - 348 BC: Olynthus sacked; the Chalcidice made part of Macedon (010.8)
  - 342–340 BC: Aristotle teaches Alexander at Mieza; the taming of Bucephalus is told here (010.11, 010.12)
  - 342–339 BC: Philip conquers Thrace; its new cities in the Hebrus valley (010.27)
  - 340 BC: Philip besieges Perinthus and Byzantium; Alexander, sixteen, is regent and subdues the Maedi (010.9, 010.28)
  - autumn 339 BC: Philip marches south and seizes Elatea (010.29)
  - August 338 BC: Chaeronea (010.13–010.17)
  - late 338 BC: Philip settles Greece treaty by treaty (Ellis ch. 15 §IX)
  - 337 BC: the congress at Corinth makes Philip hegemon and votes war on Persia (010.18)
  - spring 336 BC: the advance force crosses into Asia under Parmenion and Attalus, to Abydus (010.19, 010.30)
  - October 336 BC: Philip killed at Aegae by Pausanias; Alexander acclaimed king at about twenty (010.20–010.22, 010.31)
- Introduces: people: Perdiccas III, Philip II, Bardylis, Olympias, Alexander, Aristotle, Philoneicus,
  Bucephalus, Onomarchus, Chares and Lysicles (Athenian generals at Chaeronea; plain "Chares" is the Athenian general), Lycurgus, Parmenion, Attalus,
  Antipater, Pausanias, Cleopatra, Alexander of Epirus, Demosthenes (on the chaeronea page), Theagenes (on the Chaeronea card). States and peoples: Macedon and the Macedonians, the
  Illyrians, the Thracians, the Maedi, the Chalcidian League (Olynthus), the Thessalians, the Phocians, Epirus,
  Athens, Thebes and the Boeotians, the League of Corinth, Sparta (on the map), the Persian Empire (on the map,
  and as the enemy the League votes to punish). Places: Pella, Lake Lychnitis, Amphipolis, Crenides/Philippi,
  Mount Pangaeum, the Chalcidice, Olynthus, Thessaly, the Crocus Field, Mieza, Thrace, Philippopolis, Perinthus,
  Byzantium, Thermopylae and Elatea (on the chaeronea page), Chaeronea, the Cadmeia, Corinth, the Hellespont, Abydus, Aegae.
- Battles: Chaeronea, card, 0 phase pages (010.14). The Crocus Field is told without a card (decisions.md).
- Hands on: Alexander is king of Macedon, acclaimed by the army that Antipater presented him to. Macedon holds
  Upper and Lower Macedon, Amphipolis, Philippi and the Chalcidice, and Thrace to the Haemus as a subject
  territory; the free Thracians hold the Haemus. Philip was archon of Thessaly and hegemon of the League of
  Corinth, which covers mainland Greece except Sparta; both offices now have to pass to Alexander. An advance
  force under Parmenion and Attalus stands in Asia at the Hellespont. Unsettled: who stood behind Pausanias
  (010.21, disputed); Alexander's rivals at home and Attalus in Asia; whether the Greeks, the Thracians and the
  Illyrians will keep to what they gave Philip. 020 opens on these.

## Pages

**philip-takes-the-throne**, 360–358 BC. Perdiccas III marches against Bardylis's Illyrians and falls with more
than 4,000 men; his brother Philip, back from hostage years, takes a kingdom with Illyrians in its upland valleys.
Philip drills the army and, as Bosworth describes it, forms a closer, deeper phalanx armed with the sarissa; he
trains the cavalry. In 358 he meets
Bardylis with 10,000 foot and 600 horse, beats him, and pushes the frontier to Lake Lychnitis. Why: the Illyrian
defeat left the kingdom open to the Illyrians and the Paeonians, and to pretenders. Map: the kingdom of Macedon
appears between the Illyrians and the Thracians, with Upper Macedon secured to Lake Lychnitis. The text says once
for the chapter that its borders are approximate.
Rests on: 010.1, 010.2, 010.3, 010.4, 010.23, 010.24, 010.26
How: 010.3 is `disputed`, so it goes into the page's Notes (rules.md): the body says in words true for every account
that Philip trained and enlarged the army, with a marker; the note gives Diodorus, who makes Philip the inventor of
the phalanx, Bosworth, who calls the 12-cubit sarissa "highly probable" as Philip's innovation and describes the
closer, deeper formation, and Ellis, who sets the expansion of the army above any single invention. 010.2, 010.4 and 010.24 (more than 7,000 Illyrians killed) name Diodorus with their
figures. The battle of 358 is fought near Heraclea Lyncestis (010.23); the text may name the place, the map does
not mark it.

**amphipolis-and-philippi**, 357–356 BC. Philip takes Amphipolis by siege, makes an alliance with Olynthus and
hands it Potidaea, then answers the call of the miners of Crenides, refounds it with Macedonian settlers as
Philippi, and works the gold of Mount Pangaeum for more than 1,000 talents a year. In July 356 Olympias bears
him a son, Alexander. Why: Amphipolis was the key to the lower Strymon and Thrace, and Philip bought the
Chalcidian League's friendship while Athens was tied down in its own war with its allies. Map: Macedon reaches
across the lower Strymon to Amphipolis; Philippi stands beyond it as a Macedonian outpost; the Chalcidice stays
with Olynthus.
Rests on: 010.5, 010.6, 010.10, 010.25
How: 010.6 names Diodorus with the figure. No claim of the page is disputed, so it has no Notes unless the writer
tells Plutarch's story of three tidings reaching Philip at once with the birth; then Ellis's doubt (the birth in
July, the Olympic games in August or September) goes into Notes (rules.md) with a marker in the body.

**thessaly-and-olynthus**, 352–348 BC. Called in by the Thessalian League against the tyrants of Pherae and their
Phocian allies, Philip defeats Onomarchus at the Crocus Field in early summer 352: more than 6,000 Phocians and
mercenaries killed, 3,000 taken, Onomarchus dead. The Thessalians elect him their archon. In 348, after Olynthus
turns to Athens, he takes the city by treachery, sells its people, and makes the Chalcidice part of Macedon. Why:
Thessaly guarded the southern approach to Macedon and gave Philip its cavalry; Olynthus had broken with him. Map:
Thessaly comes under Philip; the Chalcidice and the coast between the Strymon and Philippi join Macedon; the
Olynthian zone is gone.
Rests on: 010.7, 010.8
How: the figures of 010.7 are Diodorus's. The text says the Crocus Field was probably fought on the coastal plain
west of the Gulf of Pagasae (Ellis), and the marker says "probably".

**thrace-and-the-young-alexander**, 342–339 BC. Philip spends two years conquering the Thracian kingdoms of the
Hebrus valley and founds Philippopolis; Thrace becomes a subject territory paying tribute. At Mieza, from 342,
Aristotle teaches Alexander in the precinct of the Nymphs; Plutarch's story of the boy who tames Bucephalus,
the Thessalian horse offered to Philip for thirteen talents, is told here. In 340 Philip besieges Perinthus and
then Byzantium, both helped by Byzantium's allies and by Persian satraps, and fails at both; at home Alexander,
sixteen, is regent and holds the royal seal, puts down the Maedi on the upper Strymon and founds Alexandropolis
among them (site unknown). Why: Thrace gave Macedon men and tribute and the road to the Hellespont; Perinthus and
Byzantium, turned against him, held the straits. Map: Thrace to the Haemus joins Macedon; the Persian Empire
appears across the Propontis; Philip's march to the Hebrus, Perinthus and Byzantium, and Alexander's march
against the Maedi.
Rests on: 010.9, 010.11, 010.12, 010.27, 010.28
How: 010.9 (the regency), 010.11 and 010.12 are `single`: the text gives them as Plutarch tells them. The route of
010.28 is Ellis's alone. 010.27 gives no line for the Haemus: the text says the northern border is approximate.
Passages with no digest line, told only as background and drawn nowhere: the Persian help to Perinthus (Ellis
ch. 15 §VII; Diodorus book 16, ch. 75) and the date of Aristotle's coming, summer 342 (Ellis ch. 15 §IV end;
Bosworth ch. 1 §II, "at the age of fourteen").

**chaeronea**, autumn 339 – August 338 BC. Named by the Amphictyons to fight a new sacred war, Philip marches south,
bypasses Thermopylae by Heraclea in Trachis and Cytinium, and seizes Elatea. Demosthenes brings Thebes into
alliance with Athens; the allies hold the passes over the winter, and when Parmenion breaks through to Amphissa
they fall back to Chaeronea. In August 338 Philip, with more than 30,000 foot and at least 2,000 horse, breaks
them; Alexander, on one wing, is first through the enemy line and, says Plutarch, first to break the Theban
Sacred Band. More than 1,000 Athenians fall and 2,000 are taken. Philip makes peace with Athens, but Thebes must
ransom its dead and captives and take a Macedonian garrison in the Cadmeia, its citadel; the text gives the
garrison a clause, since 020 opens with the Thebans besieging it. Why: Thebes and Athens joined to stop Philip's
march into central Greece. Map: Philip's two marches south, the allies' march to Chaeronea, the battle card;
Athens, Thebes and the other Greek states each shown.
Rests on: 010.13, 010.14, 010.15, 010.16, 010.17, 010.29, 010.32
How: 010.14 is `open`: the text says the course of the battle cannot be recovered (Ellis) and tells only
Diodorus's two wings; it gives no feigned withdrawal and no wheel. 010.15 is Plutarch's, and the text says Diodorus
has Alexander first through the line without naming the Band. 010.16 and 010.17 name Diodorus. The route of 010.29
is Ellis's alone. Parmenion's break through to Amphissa has no digest line (Ellis ch. 15 §VIII): the text may give
it in a clause, the map does not draw it. The garrison in the Cadmeia rests on 010.32 (agreed: Ellis, Diodorus 16.87.3,
Justin 9.4), and 020.8 finds it there in 335.

**hegemon-and-aegae**, 338–336 BC. Philip settles Greece treaty by treaty, garrisons Corinth, and summons the Greeks
to Corinth; all come but Sparta. The congress swears a common peace, elects Philip hegemon and votes war on
Persia to punish the burning of the temples 150 years before. In spring 336 an advance force under Parmenion and
Attalus crosses the Hellespont to Abydus and pushes south along the coast. In October 336, at Aegae, at the
wedding of his daughter Cleopatra to Alexander of Epirus, Philip enters the theatre between the two Alexanders
and is stabbed by Pausanias of his bodyguard; the assassin is cut down. Antipater presents Alexander, about
twenty, to the army, which acclaims him king. Why: the League gave Philip the Greeks' war and their levies; the
murder's motive is disputed. Map: the League of Corinth covers mainland Greece; Sparta stands outside; the advance
force crosses into Asia; Aegae marked.
Rests on: 010.18, 010.19, 010.20, 010.21, 010.22, 010.30, 010.31
How: 010.21 is `disputed`, so it goes into the page's Notes (rules.md): the body says that who stood behind
Pausanias is disputed, with a marker; the note gives Diodorus (Pausanias's private grudge against Attalus, which
Philip would not punish), Justin (the belief that Olympias set him on and Alexander knew), and Ellis (the motive
seemed inexplicable and explanations multiplied), each with its author, and settles none. The figure of 010.30, 10,000
men, names Ellis. October is "probable" (010.31) and the text keeps the word.

## Left out
Every event line of the digest's 010 is on a page. No line is left out.

Beyond the digest, these events in the sources get no page and no clause: the Paeonian campaign of 359 and
Philip's marriages before Olympias (Ellis ch. 14 §III), the Third Sacred War before 352 and its end in 346 with
the Peace of Philocrates (Ellis ch. 14 §§VI, IX), Philip's Scythian campaign of 339 and his wound among the
Triballi (Ellis ch. 15 §VIII; Justin 9.2–3), the Illyrian campaign of 337 (Ellis ch. 15 §X, of doubted
authenticity), and the Pixodarus affair and the quarrel at Philip's wedding to Cleopatra, Attalus's niece
(Bosworth ch. 1 §II; Ellis calls most of these stories later propaganda). None changes the map of a page, and the
brief does not ask for them. The writer of hegemon-and-aegae may name Attalus's quarrel only as part of
Diodorus's account of Pausanias's motive (010.21).

## Battles

**Chaeronea** (`chaeronea-338`), August 338 BC (the Attic date 7 Metageitnion, Ellis), in the plain of Chaeronea
in Boeotia.
- Sides: Macedon (`macedon`) against Athens (`athens`) and Thebes with the Boeotians (`thebes`).
- Commanders: Philip II; Alexander on one wing "with his most seasoned generals" (Diodorus 16.86.1; Bosworth says
  the left, Ellis says he probably led the Companion cavalry). Athens: Chares and Lysicles (Diodorus 16.85.2).
  Thebes: Theagenes (single, Plutarch 12.5; fell in the battle).
- Strengths: Macedon more than 30,000 foot and at least 2,000 horse (Diodorus 16.85.5; Bosworth repeats it).
  Athens and Thebes: `unknown` (Diodorus says only that Philip had the advantage in numbers).
- Casualties: Athens more than 1,000 dead and at least 2,000 captured (Diodorus 16.86.5, and Lycurgus's speech
  as Diodorus quotes it at 16.88.2; Ellis repeats them). Thebes and the Boeotians: `unknown` ("many" killed and
  "not a few" taken, Diodorus 16.86.6; "massive casualties", Ellis). Macedon: `unknown`.
- Outcome: Macedonian victory; the allied army breaks and gathers at Lebadea (Ellis); Philip garrisons the
  Cadmeia of Thebes and makes peace with Athens.
- Phase pages: none. The course is `open` (010.14): Ellis calls it "virtually impossible to recover", and the
  modern reconstructions rest on Polyaenus, not read (decisions.md, step 4). The card draws no positions.

The Crocus Field (352 BC) gets no card (decisions.md): its page tells it with a marker.

## Choices

- Six pages, as the brief expects. thessaly-and-olynthus puts the Crocus Field (352) and Olynthus (348) on one
  page: two places beside Macedon, four years apart, each a change of the same frontier; apart they would make two
  thin pages.
- chaeronea and hegemon-and-aegae stand apart, although the battle and the League fall in Greece within a year: the
  brief draws Greece twice, the coalition of 338 (athens-338, thebes-338, greeks-338) and the League of 337
  (league-337, sparta-337), and one page cannot show both. The League page also carries the advance into Asia.
- Philip's death has no page of its own: it moves no border. It shares the last page with the League and the
  advance force, the events of the same year that set up what Alexander inherits.
- Alexander's birth goes on amphipolis-and-philippi, by its date. Bucephalus, which no source dates, goes with
  Aristotle and the regency on thrace-and-the-young-alexander, the page of Alexander's youth.
- A new zone, macedon-356, sits between the brief's macedon-359 and macedon-348. Without it olynthus-356 has no
  page: macedon-348 covers the Chalcidice, and macedon-359 leaves out Amphipolis. Philippi stays outside
  macedon-356, because Ellis says it was not annexed at first; it is inside macedon-348, when the coast to
  Neapolis was Macedonian.
- macedon-359 is drawn as the kingdom after the victory of 358, to Lake Lychnitis, and philip-takes-the-throne is
  dated 360–358 BC. Ellis says the Illyrians held the northern valleys of Upper Macedon at the accession. The map shows what
  Philip made of the kingdom, not the year he lost it.
- persia-336 appears from 340 BC (thrace-and-the-young-alexander), as the brief sets it; it is named "Persian
  Empire" with no king. chaeronea leaves it off, because its frame does not reach Asia.
- The battle of 358 against Bardylis gets no marker or route, though 010.23 places it near Heraclea Lyncestis:
  the page's map change is the frontier at Lake Lychnitis, and the page keeps two markers.
- Philip's march of 339 is drawn through the places Ellis names (Heraclea in Trachis, Cytinium, Elatea). Parmenion's
  break through to Amphissa is told in the text without a route, so the page keeps three routes.
- The allies' march to Chaeronea is drawn as one route in the `athens` colour. The Thebans march from home and need
  none.
- Pella is the one marker with no digest line: it stands as Philip's capital (Ellis ch. 14 §§I, V) and is the start
  of the routes. Lake Lychnitis (010.23), Philippopolis (010.27), Elatea (010.29) and Abydus (010.30) rest on digest
  lines. Alexandropolis is not marked: its site is unknown.
- The advance force's route stops a short way south of Abydus: Ellis says it pushed south down the coast, and no
  source says how far by October 336.
- No route on thessaly-and-olynthus or amphipolis-and-philippi: the sources give no line of march for 357–348
  beyond the places themselves.
