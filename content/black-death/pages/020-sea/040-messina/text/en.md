In early October 1347, by the account of the Franciscan friar Michele da Piazza, twelve Genoese galleys from the east put in at [Messina](@messina), the port on the strait between Sicily and Calabria. Michele writes his history of Sicily some years later. “In their bones they bore so virulent a disease,” he writes, “that anyone who only spoke to them was seized by a mortal illness and in no manner could evade death.”

The people of Messina drive the galleys out, but the plague stays in the city. Soon no doctor, notary or priest will go near the sick, because those who came to treat them, write their wills or hear their confessions have caught the disease. With the guards and officials dying too, nobody stops the thieves who loot the empty houses of those who have fled.

Many people of Messina go south to [Catania](@catania), said to be the second city of the island, trusting in its patron saint, Agatha. In November, Michele writes, the archbishop of Catania gives in to their pleas to have her relics carried to Messina, but the people of Catania will not let the saint’s bones leave the town. The plague follows the refugees, and many die in Catania’s hospitals. At the townspeople’s demand the archbishop forbids, on pain of excommunication, the burial of anyone from Messina inside the walls, and their bodies go into pits outside. Duke John of Randazzo, who governs Sicily for his nephew, the nine-year-old King Louis, flees Catania for the forest of Mascali, and dies of the plague there in April 1348.

By November the plague is all over Sicily, carried by people who leave Messina in every direction, many of whom die on the way. By the end of 1347 it has probably crossed the strait into Calabria. The galleys driven from Messina sail on, and in the following weeks ships bring the plague to the ports of the western Mediterranean.
