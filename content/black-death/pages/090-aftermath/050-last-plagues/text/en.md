Two of the last great outbreaks of plague in western Europe strike [London](@london-1665) in 1665 and [Marseille](@marseille-1720) in 1720.

In London three cases are recorded in April 1665, and the government orders the houses of the sick shut up with their families inside. A red cross and the words “Lord, have mercy upon us” mark the door. Once the plague has taken hold of the City in July, the king and his court leave for Salisbury and then Oxford. “Lord! How empty the streets are and how melancholy”, Samuel Pepys writes in his diary. The bills of mortality count 97,306 burials in 1665, 68,596 of them of the plague. Many families hide a plague death, so the true toll is higher: the London Museum puts it at about 100,000, a fifth of Londoners. In the Derbyshire village of Eyam, where by local tradition the plague comes with a parcel of cloth from London, the villagers shut themselves off from their neighbours, and about a third of them die.

On 25 May 1720 the merchant ship Grand-Saint-Antoine reaches Marseille with silk and cotton loaded at [Sidon](@sidon-1720). On the way it calls at Tyre, Tripoli, Cyprus, Le Brusc near Toulon and Livorno, which turns it away. A Turkish passenger, several of the crew and the ship’s surgeon die during the voyage. The town’s health officers let it through, probably to save a cargo meant for the fair at Beaucaire, and on 20 June the town has its first death. Of the town’s 90,000 people, 40,000 die by 1722 by the count of Charles Carrière and his co-authors, and 50,000 with its countryside. The Parlement of Aix forbids all contact with Marseille on pain of death, and a dry stone wall, the Mur de la peste, is built across the Vaucluse plateau to hold the plague back.

Moscow suffers a great plague in 1771, and the plague returns to the Ottoman lands until the 19th century, when the second pandemic, which began with the Black Death, fades out.
