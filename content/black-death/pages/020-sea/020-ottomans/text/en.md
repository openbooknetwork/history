Across the Bosporus from [Constantinople](@constantinople) lie the lands of Orhan, the second ruler of the Ottomans. From his capital, [Bursa](@bursa), he holds [İznik](@iznik-1347), taken in 1331, [İzmit](@izmit-1347), taken in 1337, and the southern and eastern shores of the Sea of Marmara. In 1346 he marries Theodora, a daughter of John Kantakouzenos, and Ottoman troops fight for Kantakouzenos in Thrace.

The plague probably crosses to these lands from Constantinople and the ports of the Marmara in 1347, but the sources say almost nothing of it. The Ottoman chronicles, written a century later, do not mention it, by one account, and no figure of the dead survives for Bursa or İznik. Orhan’s state left a thin written record: the history that Yahşi Fakih wrote, the main source of all later Ottoman chronicles for these early years, is lost. Across the sea in Thrace, the plagues of 1348 and 1362 strike down the peasants and the townspeople.

The historian Uli Schamiloglu argues that the plague struck the people of crowded towns harder than nomads who moved with their herds. In his view it weakened the Byzantine towns of the region more than the Turkmen around them, and so it helped the Ottomans grow.

Muslim jurists teach a saying of the Prophet Muhammad on plague: when you hear of it in a land, do not go there, and when it breaks out in the land where you are, do not leave. They also teach that a believer who dies of plague dies a martyr. Nearly five centuries later, in 1838, the Ottoman government founds a Quarantine Administration in Istanbul, part of a new system of permanent quarantines in the eastern Mediterranean.
