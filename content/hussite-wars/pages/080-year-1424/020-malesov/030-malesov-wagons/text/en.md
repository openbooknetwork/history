When about half of the Prague column has crossed the brook and begins to climb towards him, Žižka has the stone wagons let go. They run down the slope into the head of the column. Men on foot cannot stand against them, and the narrow valley leaves no room to step aside, so the ranks break, and the guns fire into the confusion.

Only the younger Old Czech Annals, written down long after, tell of the stone wagons, and Josef Pekař and later historians doubt the story. Aeneas Silvius and the Breslau manuscript speak only of a fight in the valley, and Petr Čornej reads the sources as a sudden blow against the head of the column, strung out by the fast march and the narrow ground. Jan Biederman of the Military History Institute in Prague suggests that the story grew from a picture in Konrad Kyeser’s Bellifortis of about 1405, where a soldier is about to push a wagon of stones down onto armed men marching through a ravine.
