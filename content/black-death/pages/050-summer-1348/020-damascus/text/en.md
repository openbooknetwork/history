From Egypt the plague travels along the coast road into Syria, which the Mamluk sultans rule from Cairo. It reaches [Gaza](@gaza) in April or May 1348, and from there it spreads to [Jerusalem](@jerusalem) and inland to [Damascus](@damascus) and the towns on the road north.

Ibn Battuta, the traveller from Tangier, is on his way home after more than twenty years of travel. At [Aleppo](@aleppo) in early June 1348 he hears that the plague has broken out at Gaza and that more than a thousand people are dying there each day. At [Homs](@homs) about three hundred die on the day he arrives. He reaches Damascus in the second half of July, when up to 2,400 people are dying there each day. The governor, Arghun Shah, orders three days of fasting. On the last night people fill the Umayyad Mosque to pray, and on Friday morning they walk out together to the [Mosque of the Footprints](@damascus-procession), two miles south of the city. “The Jews came with their Book of the Law and the Christians with their Gospel, all of them with their women and children,” Ibn Battuta wrote. Later he passes through Gaza again and finds most of the town deserted. Its judge tells him that 1,100 people a day had died there, and that only a quarter of its eighty notaries are still alive.

At Aleppo the scholar Ibn al-Wardi writes an essay on the plague while it rages. He traces its course from China and India to Syria. The essay holds two views side by side, although they do not agree: the plague is a martyrdom for the Muslims who die and a punishment for unbelievers, and it is also part of God’s punishment for sins. Ibn al-Wardi dies of the plague at Aleppo on 18 March 1349.
