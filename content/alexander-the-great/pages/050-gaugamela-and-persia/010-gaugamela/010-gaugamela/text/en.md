In spring 331 BC Alexander leaves [Memphis](@memphis-331b) and marches back to [Tyre](@tyre-331). Darius III, the Great King of Persia, still rules every land east of the Euphrates. At [Babylon](@babylon-331) he has gathered a new army from the eastern satrapies, and its best troops are the horsemen of Bactria under their satrap, Bessus.

In summer 331 BC Darius makes his greatest offer: all the land west of the Euphrates, one of his daughters in marriage, and a ransom for his family, captured at Issus.\* Parmenion, Alexander’s senior general, is said to have told him that he would accept if he were Alexander. “So should I, if I were Parmenion,” Alexander answers in Diodorus’s account. He wants the whole empire, and tells Darius to come and submit.

Arrian writes that Darius had been talked into blaming his defeat at Issus on the narrow field. He marches north from Babylon, crosses the Tigris, passes Arbela and halts at Gaugamela by the river Bumelus. There he has the plain levelled for his cavalry and his 200 scythed chariots and waits.

In July or August Alexander reaches [Thapsacus](@thapsacus-331) on the Euphrates, whose site is lost. Mazaeus, guarding the river for Darius, falls back as the army comes near, and the Macedonians cross on two bridges. They march east across northern Mesopotamia and in September ford the Tigris unopposed. On the evening of 20 September, a Babylonian astronomical diary records, the moon is eclipsed.

Arrian gives Alexander 7,000 cavalry and about 40,000 infantry, and the Persian army is far larger.\*\* On 1 October 331 BC the two armies meet on the levelled plain. The historian Ernst Badian judges it the last full battle that Alexander fights against the empire’s grand army. The borders and the lines of march on the maps of this chapter are approximate.

### Notes

\* Diodorus and Bosworth put the ransom at 30,000 talents, Arrian and Plutarch at 10,000. Diodorus, Plutarch and Bosworth date this offer to summer 331 BC; Arrian alone sets it earlier, during the siege of Tyre in 332 BC.

\*\* The ancient figures for Darius’s army run from 245,000 men in Curtius, 45,000 horse and 200,000 foot, to more than a million in Arrian. Bosworth holds that the Persian advantage cannot be counted.
