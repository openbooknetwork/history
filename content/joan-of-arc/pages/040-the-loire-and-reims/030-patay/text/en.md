At about eight on the morning of Saturday 18 June the English at [Meung](@meung-18-june-1429) learn that [Beaugency](@beaugency-18-june-1429) has fallen. They give up Meung and march for Janville, with the English main body under Sir John Fastolf and Lord Talbot. Joan tells the captains to have good spurs, for the English will not stand.

La Hire and Poton de Xaintrailles lead the French vanguard. The main body follows under the Duke of Alençon and the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois). Joan, kept out of the van, is much vexed. A stag breaks from the woods into the English ranks, and their shout gives them away to the French scouts.

Talbot dismounts with 500 picked archers to hold a passage between two hedges near Patay. The French are on him before the English are formed, and before those on foot have planted their stakes. Talbot is overrun, and the English van, seeing Fastolf ride towards it, flees. It is over at about two in the afternoon.

Talbot is taken, by Xaintrailles's archers, and so are the English captains Lord Scales and Sir Thomas Rempston. The French lose few men. Fastolf escapes to [Étampes](@etampes-1429) and then Corbeil; the regent, Bedford, rebukes him, and he loses the Garter. Joan gets down from her horse to hold the head of an English prisoner struck down by his captor, and has him confessed.

[Janville](@janville-shut) shuts its gates on the fugitives and gives itself to the king. The English burn Montpipeau, Saint-Sigismond and other strongholds and flee. In a week they have lost every place on the Loire about Orléans, and the Beauce is clear almost to Paris. On 19 June Joan and Alençon bring the prisoners back to [Orléans](@orleans-19-june-1429).

[Chartres](@chartres-1429) and Étampes stay English.
