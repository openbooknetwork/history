Alexander's empire has broken into kingdoms, but some of his cities outlive it. The first and greatest is [Alexandria](@alexandria-301) in Egypt, which he laid out himself as a Greek city and which now belongs to Ptolemy.

In the east many of his cities were garrisons. [Alexandria in Areia (today Herat)](@alexandria-areia-301) was founded in 330 BC after a revolt there, and [Alexandria in Arachosia](@alexandria-arachosia-301), which may be today's Kandahar, perhaps early in 329 BC. Only for [Alexandria in the Caucasus (near Begram)](@alexandria-caucasus-301), where the routes across the Hindu Kush meet, do figures survive: Alexander settled 3,000 Greeks and Macedonians there, veterans unfit to fight among them, and 7,000 local people to farm for them. [Alexandria Eschate (today Khujand)](@alexandria-eschate-301) guarded the Jaxartes against the nomads beyond it. The sites of Bucephala and Nicaea, on the Hydaspes, are lost. Many settlers stayed only out of fear: when Alexander died, more than 20,000 of them, Diodorus says, set out for home and were massacred.

How many cities Alexander founded is disputed.\* In Bosworth's judgement several of the Alexandrias of Asia Minor and the Near East are the work of the Successors, and [Alexandria Troas](@alexandria-troas-301) at least was refounded by Lysimachus.

The Successors' kingdoms carried Greek far beyond the old Greek world. The editors of the Cambridge Ancient History call the common Greek of the age, the koine, "almost a universal language", although no political or economic union came with it.

Alexander lives on in legend too. In the Alexander Romance, a life of him built on a few facts and filled out with marvels, a son of Antipater poisons him. Bosworth traces such tales of his death back to the propaganda of the Successors' wars over his empire.
