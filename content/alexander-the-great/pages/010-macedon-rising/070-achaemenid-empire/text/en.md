In 336 BC, the year Alexander becomes king of Macedon, Persia also gets a new ruler. Two years earlier the Great King Artaxerxes III was poisoned by Bagoas, the eunuch who was his grand vizier, and the king's youngest son Arses was raised to the throne. Now Arses is murdered with his children, and Darius, of a side branch of the royal family by the descent Diodorus gives him, becomes king.\* Diodorus tells that Bagoas then tried to poison Darius and was made to drain the cup himself. By summer 334 BC Darius III has won back Egypt and has 400 warships ready for the Aegean.

His empire reaches from Asia Minor and [Egypt](@egypt-336) to [Bactria](@bactria-336) in Central Asia. It is divided into some twenty provinces, called satrapies, each under a governor, the satrap. Ancient writers give only pieces of their borders. The historian Bruno Jacobs works the satrapies out from the lists of provinces that Alexander's generals shared out after his death, since Alexander had kept the Persian divisions almost everywhere. The map draws no borders between them and names a few: [Hellespontine Phrygia](@hellespontine-phrygia-336) facing Greece, Egypt, [Babylonia](@babylonia-336), [Persis](@persis-336), the Persians' homeland, [Media](@media-336) and Bactria.

A road runs from [Sardis](@sardis-336), the seat of the satrap of Lydia, to [Susa](@susa-336), the Great King's royal city. The Greek historian Herodotus gives this Royal Road 13,500 stadia and ninety days' march; Pierre Briant, a historian of the Persian Empire, makes that about 2,500 km. Briant warns that Herodotus's account of the roads is fragmentary, and the course on the map is approximate.

Greek writers said that the Great King could no longer field a real army and had to lean on Greek mercenaries. Briant traces this picture to Diodorus, who drew on the anti-Persian historian Ephorus, and holds it untenable.

### Notes

\* Diodorus makes Bagoas the kingmaker: having killed Arses, he chose Darius, a man of the court, expecting to rule through him. Bosworth and Badian follow this account. Briant warns against giving Diodorus's all-powerful eunuch too much weight. In his view Darius, who had belonged to the king's inner circle for years, was the real head of the plot and Bagoas only his helper, whom Darius removed once he was king.
