On Holy Saturday, 22 March 1421, the Dauphin's army of Scots and French under the Earl of Buchan lies at Baugé in Anjou, having come up by Le Lude the day before. Thomas, Duke of Clarence, Henry V's brother, hears of it at dinner at Beaufort-en-Vallée and rides out at once with his mounted men-at-arms, without waiting for his archers. Wylie and Waugh place the fight beyond the bridge over the Couasnon, on a low hill by the church of Vieil-Baugé. Clarence is killed, and the Earls of Somerset and Huntingdon are taken. It is the first defeat of the English in the field in this story, won by the army that Cravant and Verneuil will break.\*

Henry V besieges [Meaux](@meaux-1422) on the Marne from October 1421 until May 1422, and there, Oman writes, he catches the dysentery that kills him. He dies in the castle of [Vincennes](@vincennes-1422) on 31 August 1422. Henry VI, his son by Catherine of Valois, is a baby of nine months, and Henry V's brother John, Duke of Bedford, governs France for him as regent. Charles VI dies in [Paris](@paris-1422) on 21 October. At his burial at [Saint-Denis](@saint-denis-1422) on 11 November a herald cries Henry VI King of France and England, and Bedford rides back to Paris with the king's sword carried before him.

The Dauphin Charles has not waited: on 30 October, as Beaucourt tells it, he has already taken the title of King at [Mehun-sur-Yèvre](@mehun-1422), and from here this story calls him Charles VII. His enemies call him the "King of Bourges", because, Beaucourt explains, he stays so long at [Bourges](@bourges-1422).

So France has two kings. Henry VI holds the north, where Lowell, for 1428, lists Paris and its country, Normandy, Perche, Alençon and most of Maine and Champagne. Charles VII is obeyed south of the Loire, between the Garonne and the Rhône, and in Dauphiné. The map shows the two roads to Baugé and the places of the two deaths and the two proclamations. Its zones are still those of the Treaty of Troyes, and approximate.

### Notes

\* The sources count the two armies at Baugé differently. Wylie and Waugh give Clarence 1,500 men-at-arms, from a record of the time, and report that Jean Juvénal des Ursins, a chronicler, put the French and Scots at 5,000 to 6,000. Oman has the Scots and French outnumbering Clarence's horsemen two to one.
