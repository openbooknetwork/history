By the end of 1353 the plague has killed between 24 and 46 million people in the lands on this map, by the figure at the top. The edge of the land it reached is approximate, because each town with a known date is given the land nearer to it than to any other town. Historians rebuild the toll from records kept for other purposes.

Earlier pages give some of these records: the taxpayers of [Asyut](@asyut), the bishops’ registers of [England](@dead-england), and the book of citizens of [Bremen](@bremen-1350) with its 6,966 dead. At [Givry](@givry-1348) in Burgundy the parish buries perhaps as many people in under four months of 1348 as in the twenty years before, 615 by Pierre Gras’s count of its register. In [Florence](@florence-1348) Boccaccio counts more than 100,000 dead, close to the whole city of about 120,000.

Where few records survive, as around [Prague](@prague) and in [Poland](@dead-poland), the toll is hard to measure, and the figure’s range there is wide.

For each region the plague has reached, the figure multiplies its people about 1346 by the share that dies, low and high, and by the part of its epidemic past by the page’s date. Most regions of Europe take a third as the low share, the estimate of older histories, and the 60 percent of the historian Ole Benedictow as the high. [Egypt](@dead-egypt) takes a third to half, as the historian Michael Dols estimates. The rates of [Syria](@dead-syria), a quarter to 38 percent, and of the [Maghreb](@dead-maghreb), a quarter to a third, are this book’s own assumptions, set around Dols’s third for the Middle East. Central Asia, the Golden Horde, Iraq and Arabia stay out of the sum, because no figure for their people survives.

For Europe alone, historians give 25 to 50 million dead, a third to 60 percent of the 80 million people Benedictow counts. The map’s regions hold about 72 million Europeans, so its count for Europe is lower.
