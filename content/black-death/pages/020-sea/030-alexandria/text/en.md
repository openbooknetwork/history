In the autumn of 1347 a ship comes into the harbour of [Alexandria](@alexandria), the main port of the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt and Syria. The Cairo historian al-Maqrizi, who wrote in the 15th century, is said to count 32 merchants and about 300 people in all aboard, traders and slaves among them. Nearly all are dead when the ship drifts in. Four merchants, one slave and about 40 sailors reach the port alive, and they die in the city soon after. The plague then kills a hundred people a day in Alexandria, al-Maqrizi writes, and later two hundred.

The ship came from the north, from the Black Sea or [Constantinople](@constantinople), on the sea lanes that bring the sultans their slaves. Every year, one account has it, Genoese ships from the Crimea carry boys bought in the lands of the Golden Horde to Egypt, where they are trained as soldiers. The emirs who govern Egypt began their lives as such slaves.

From Alexandria the plague spreads into the Nile delta, by one account along its canals and the two branches of the river, and up the Nile towards [Cairo](@cairo-1347). By the spring of 1348 it is everywhere in the north of Egypt. In the delta, al-Maqrizi writes, “no one was left to gather the crops.” Ships on the same sea lanes probably carry it to Cyprus.

The plague comes to a Mamluk state that has just lost its strongest ruler. Al-Nasir Muhammad died in 1341, and in the six years since then six of his sons have sat on the throne, most of them puppets of the emirs. In December 1347 the emirs make his son Hasan sultan, a boy of about 12.
