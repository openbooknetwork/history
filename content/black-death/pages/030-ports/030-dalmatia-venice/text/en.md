In the autumn of 1347 the Venetian galley fleet comes home from Caffa. It sails north along the coast of Dalmatia and leaves the plague in the ports where it calls. At [Split](@split) an anonymous chronicler of the town dates the outbreak to Christmas Day 1347. [Ragusa](@ragusa), today Dubrovnik, notes it before 13 January 1348, and the other ports of the coast have it around the turn of the year.

[Venice](@venice), among the largest cities of Europe, has the plague in January 1348. A later page tells how its government answers, with a health commission and burial grounds on the islands of the lagoon.

On 25 January 1348 an earthquake shakes Friuli, Carinthia and the north of Italy. Part of the patriarch’s palace in Udine falls, and so do churches and houses in [Villach](@villach-1348). A landslide from the Dobratsch mountain dams the river Gail, and when the dam breaks the flood damages several villages and Villach itself. Chroniclers often tell the earthquake and the plague together, but few of them call the earthquake a punishment from God. The one plain word on the end of the world comes from Giovanni Villani in Florence, who follows a letter from Florentine merchants. In his Book of Nature, written in 1349, the scholar Konrad of Megenberg argues that the earthquake let out vapours shut in the mountains for years, that these poisoned the air, and that the poisoned air caused the plague.
