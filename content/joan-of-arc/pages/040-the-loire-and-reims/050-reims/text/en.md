[Châlons](@chalons-1429) opens to Charles VII: its bishop and burgesses come out and give him full obedience, and he is received there on 14 July. He lodges at [Sept-Saulx](@sept-saulx-1429), a castle of Regnault de Chartres, Archbishop of Reims, four leagues from the city. The Anglo-Burgundian captains leave Reims, and its burgesses bring him the keys. On Saturday 16 July the king rides into [Reims](@reims-cathedral).

On Sunday 17 July the archbishop anoints and crowns Charles VII in the cathedral, in a service that runs from nine to two. The abbot of Saint-Remi brings the Holy Ampulla, escorted by four captains who ride armed into the church. The Duke of Alençon knights the king and stands in for the absent Duke of Burgundy.

All through the rite Joan stands beside the king with her standard in her hand. Asked later why her standard went into the church rather than the other captains', she answers: "It had been present in the perils; that was reason enough for it to be honored."\* One chronicle has her kneel in tears after the anointing and say that God's will is done; whether she says it cannot be known.

That day Joan writes to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, bidding him make peace with the king. He does not answer. His envoys come to the king at Reims and get Georges de La Trémoille, the king's favourite, to sign a truce of fifteen days. Joan is told that the duke will hand over Paris when it ends. Bedford, the English regent, will count Reims, Troyes and Châlons among the cities that went over without a fight.

The towns on the road from [Troyes](@troyes-july-1429) to Reims are now the king's.
