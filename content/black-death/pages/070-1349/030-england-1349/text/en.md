By March 1349 the plague is breaking out all over the south of England, in no clear order. It is said to reach [Norwich](@norwich) in the east during the spring, and in the summer it is at its worst in that diocese. By one account it also moves west from [Bristol](@bristol) into Wales, though its course there is little known. Ships also carry it into the Humber, and from there it spreads south and north: [York](@york) has it in May, and the north suffers worst through the three summer months. By the end of the summer it has reached the whole kingdom, and by December 1349 it has died down.

The bishops’ registers show how many priests die. A bishop records each new priest he places in a parish, so a run of new appointments marks the deaths of the old ones. From these registers the historian Josiah Russell puts the deaths among the clergy at 30 to 40 per cent, and in parts of the diocese of York the rate may reach 48 per cent. On 17 January 1349 Ralph of Shrewsbury, bishop of Bath and Wells, writes to his clergy that a dying man who can find no priest may confess to a layman, or even to a woman.

The plague kills two [archbishops of Canterbury](@ufford-bradwardine) within fourteen weeks. John de Ufford, Edward III’s chancellor, is old and sick when it carries him off on 20 May 1349, before he can be consecrated. Thomas Bradwardine, an Oxford theologian and Edward III’s confessor, is consecrated on 19 July and dies on 26 August 1349.

In 2013 archaeologists at [Thornton Abbey](@thornton-abbey) in Lincolnshire find a plague pit in the burial ground of the abbey’s hospital. At least 48 bodies lie in it, 27 of them children’s. It is the first mass grave of the Black Death found in the British countryside. Hugh Willmott’s team tests the teeth of 16 of the dead, and one carries the DNA of Yersinia pestis, the plague bacterium. The team takes the plague of 1349 as the most likely cause.
