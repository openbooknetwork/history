For eight years after 1338 no source records the plague, and its road west is unknown. The map draws it along the caravan road that links the Chüy valley, through Otrar and [Urgench](@urgench-1346), with the Volga.

In 1342 Jani Beg, a son of Khan Özbeg, becomes khan of the Golden Horde, after his followers kill his elder brother Tini Beg and he orders the death of a younger one. He rules from [Sarai](@sarai) on the lower Volga. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta, there about 1332, counts thirteen mosques for Friday prayers and finds merchants from Iraq, Egypt and Syria living in a walled quarter of their own. By one account the city also has workshops, and merchants from Italy and Rus trade in its markets.

In the spring and summer of 1346 the plague is reported in most towns of the Horde, among them Urgench, Sarai and [Azaq](@azaq-1346), the Italians’ Tana. A Russian chronicle records under the year 1346 that God’s punishment fell on the people of the eastern lands, in the towns of Ornach, which may be Urgench, Khastorokan, which is [Astrakhan](@astrakhan), Sarai and Bezdezh. It names the peoples who died there: Muslims, Tatars, Armenians, Abkhaz, Jews, European foreigners, whom it calls Fryazi, and Circassians. The Arab scholar Ibn al-Wardi, who dies of the plague at Aleppo in 1349, writes there, it is said in 1348, that it came to the land of Özbeg, meaning the Golden Horde, and left its villages and towns empty.

No one knows how many died in the Horde. It probably loses a large share of its people. Whole regions empty, the economy shrinks fast, and by the 1350s parts of the trade road north of the Caspian break down. From the second half of the century its towns decline, and in 1359, two years after Jani Beg’s death, its political order collapses. Twenty years of civil war follow.

In 1346 Jani Beg’s army is in the Crimea, besieging the Genoese port of Caffa, and the plague reaches it there.
