From her lodging in [Jacques Boucher's house](@boucher-house), says the Orléans chronicle of the feast of 8 May, Joan can see the whole siege. The English hold the city with a chain of separate forts, the bastilles, on both banks of the Loire. They cover the west and the south and leave the north-east open, so that armed companies come and go. On the north bank stand [the bastille of Saint-Laurent](@bastille-saint-laurent), the largest, outside the west gate, where the chief captains and most of the English lie; [the bastille of London](@bastille-london), [the bastille of Rouen](@bastille-rouen) and [the bastille of Paris](@bastille-paris) to the north-west and north; and Saint-Loup to the east. On the south bank are Saint-Jean-le-Blanc, the Augustins, Saint-Privé and the Tourelles, with a fort on [the Île Charlemagne](@ile-charlemagne) between.\* Suffolk has the chief command since Salisbury's death, with Lord Talbot and Lord Scales, for Bedford, the regent. William Glasdale holds the Tourelles.\*\*

On Saturday 30 April La Hire and Florent d'Illiers drive the English back from their watch at Saint-Pouair, by the bastille of Paris; Joan takes no part. The English have kept the herald who carried her letter, and send word that they will burn her, a whore who should go back to her cows. That evening she goes to [the Belle-Croix](@belle-croix), the French earthwork on the bridge facing the Tourelles across the broken arches, and calls on Glasdale and his men to yield, with their lives spared. They shout back that she is a cowherd. So say the Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of the city, and Lowell; the Chronique de la Pucelle, and Ramsay after it, put the scene on 5 May.

On 1 May the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois) rides to Blois to bring the army back. On 2 May Joan rides out to look at the English forts and hears vespers at Sainte-Croix. The army leaves Blois on 3 May by the north bank, through the Beauce, and spends a night halfway, says the Chronique. The same day the garrisons of Montargis, Gien and Château-Renard come in; Ramsay has the men of Gien and Montargis join the army on the road. On the morning of 4 May Joan rides out with about 500 men, the Journal's figure, and the army comes in past the English bastilles. Their garrisons stay inside.\*\*\* Lowell counts about 5,000 regular troops now in the city, besides several thousand armed townsmen.

The map shows the bastilles and the army's road round the north of the city. Both are approximate, since no plan of the siege survives.

### Notes

\* Ramsay counts seven works on the north bank and four on the south, with the Tourelles and the island. The chronicle of the feast gives ten or eleven bastilles, the Chronique thirteen forts and earthworks in December 1428, and Monstrelet, a Burgundian chronicler, not fewer than sixty towers and batteries.

\*\* The Chronique puts the besiegers at 10,000 under the captains Bedford sent after Salisbury's death, who closed the Beauce side in December 1428. Oman writes of an army of 5,000, and Lowell thinks the English in May hardly equal to the French regulars.

\*\*\* The chronicle of the feast and Lang name the bastille of Paris as the one the army passed closest to. The Journal and the Chronique name none.
