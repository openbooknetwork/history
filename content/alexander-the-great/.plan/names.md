# Names of Alexander the Great

"First named in" gives the section that first names it and the digest claim, or, where the digest has no claim
for it, the source passage the section's coordinator takes it from. "Other forms" are the
spellings the story's source files use; a text quotes them only inside a quote. A name marked "not in the digest's
claims" is in the source files but not yet in a claim; a writer who uses it cites the source it stands in.

## Rules

### Calendar and dates

- Dates follow the Julian calendar carried back before its use, with BC after every year: 334 BC. A century
  after the Common Era takes AD: the 1st century AD.
- A date the sources give in a Greek, Macedonian or Babylonian month is written as the Julian month the digest
  gives: Daesius 334 is May 334 BC, Maimacterion 333 is November 333 BC, Munychion 326 is May 326 BC. The source's
  month may follow in brackets only where the text explains how the date is known: "in November 333 BC (the
  Athenian month Maimacterion)".
- A day is written only where a source gives it in our calendar: the lunar eclipse of 20 September 331 BC, the
  battle of Gaugamela on 1 October 331 BC. Alexander dies "between the evenings of 10 and 11 June 323 BC" in a text,
  "June 323 BC" in a label; "11 June" alone rests on Depuydt, who was not read.
- A date known only by season takes the season: "in spring 334 BC". A winter that runs across two years takes both:
  "in winter 334–333 BC". An event known only to an Athenian year, which runs across two of ours, takes both years
  with "or": "in 338 or 337 BC". Where two sources give different dates, the text names both with who gives them:
  "Arrian dates it to spring 327 BC, Bosworth to summer 328 BC".
- An approximate year or age takes "about": about 20, about 305 BC.
- Prose writes a span as "from 336 to 323 BC"; a label writes "336–323 BC" with an en dash and no spaces.

### Names of places

- A place takes its ancient name in the English form of current scholarship: Halicarnassus, Maracanda, Ecbatana,
  Byzantium, Arbela. Today's name may follow in brackets once per page: "Ecbatana (today Hamadan)". This follows the
  Punic Wars story, which writes New Carthage and Gades, and Bosworth, who writes "Maracanda (Samarkand)".
- Exceptions are a few natural features and regions that English histories of Alexander name only by today's name:
  the Danube, the Hindu Kush, Asia Minor, the Indian Ocean. Being a lake, river or mountain does not by itself make
  a feature an exception: Lake Lychnitis (not Lake Ohrid) follows the ancient rule, because Diodorus and Ellis
  name it so and it is a frontier of Philip's time, as Lake Mareotis is. The same goes for the Antilibanus (Bosworth,
  Plutarch), the Strymon, the Axius, the Lycus and the Araxes.
- Places that share a name keep a fixed qualifier on every mention: Heraclea Lyncestis, Heraclea in Trachis,
  Heraclea by Latmus; Alexandria alone means the city in Egypt, and the others are Alexandria Eschate, Alexandria in
  Areia, Alexandria in Arachosia, Alexandria in the Caucasus and Alexandria Troas; Heliopolis is the Egyptian city;
  the Araxes is the river of Persis (the Pulvar); Paraetacae is in Sogdiana and Paraetacene in Iran; Gabae is the
  Sogdian fortress. The Greeks' word "Ocean" may be explained once on the
  page where the army reaches the sea.
- Macedon is both the kingdom and its land; "Macedonia" is used only inside a quote.
- A battle, siege or treaty keeps the name histories give it: the battle of the Granicus, the battle of Issus, the
  battle of Gaugamela (not Arbela), the battle of the Hydaspes, the battle of the Persian Gate, the siege of Tyre, the
  League of Corinth, the Lamian War, the Babylon settlement, the settlement of Triparadisus, the Susa weddings, the
  Opis mutiny, the pages' conspiracy, the wars of the Successors.
- The site of a battle that the sources do not place (the Pinarus, the Persian Gate, the Hydaspes crossing, the
  Mallian town, Aornos, Massaga) is named by its ancient name, and its "Today" stays "site unknown" or "disputed" on
  the map as in the table.

### Names of people

- A person takes the name of the time. Arrhidaeus becomes Philip III in June 323 BC. Later epithets (Monophthalmus,
  Poliorcetes, Soter, Nicator) are not used. "The Great" is used only for Cyrus, and for Alexander in the title, the
  summary, and where a text speaks of his later fame. "Cleitus the Black" tells Cleitus apart from Cleitus, son of
  Bardylis.
- A king who shares a name with another person in the story keeps his numeral at every mention: Perdiccas III,
  Philip II (except as plain "Philip" in 010–080), Philip III, Alexander IV, Darius I, Agis III, Artaxerxes III.
  "Alexander" alone means Alexander III, and "Darius" alone means Darius III.
- Where two people share a name, the one the table lists with a patronymic or a description keeps it at every
  mention: Amyntas, son of Perdiccas; Amyntas, son of Arrhabaeus; Amyntas, son of Andromenes; Amyntas, son of
  Nicolaus (no one takes the bare name Amyntas: the Amyntas of the advance force of 336 BC in 010.30 is written
  "an officer named Amyntas", because the sources do not say which he is); Attalus, son of
  Andromenes; Demetrius, son of Althaemenes; Philotas, satrap of Cilicia; Glaucias, Cassander's officer; Ptolemy,
  son of Seleucus; Philip the Acarnanian; Philip, son of Menelaus; Attalus, commander of the Agrianians; Chares of
  Mytilene. The other person of the pair takes the bare name:
  Attalus, Demetrius, Philotas, Glaucias, Ptolemy, Philip, Chares.
- Where three or more people share a name, every one of them keeps a fixed description: Nicanor, the fleet
  commander; Nicanor, son of Parmenion; Nicanor, Ptolemy's general; Menon, son of Cerdimmas; Menon the Thessalian.
  (Arrian's Menon, satrap of Arachosia, 3.28, would need his own description if a section names him.)
- A Persian king is "the Great King" as a title. "King" takes a capital only as part of a title.

### Transliteration

- Greek names take the Latinized English forms of current Alexander scholarship, Bosworth's in particular: c for
  kappa (Cleitus, Coenus, Craterus), -us for -os (Cleitus, Coenus), -ium for -ion (Gordium, Dascylium), ae and oe
  kept (Chaeronea, Aegae, Coenus), and ei where scholarship keeps it (Cleitus, Areia, Hypereides, Stateira, Peithon).
  Names with an established English form keep it: Ptolemy, Aristotle, Plutarch, Athens, Thebes, Corinth, Troy,
  Rhodes, Cyprus, Parmenion.
- Persian, Bactrian and Sogdian names take the Greek forms that English scholarship writes (Darius, Bessus,
  Spitamenes, Oxyartes). Old Persian forms are not used.
- Indian names take the Greek forms of the sources (Porus, Taxiles, Malli). Forms from later Indian sources are not
  used.
- Gods and heroes: Ammon for the god of Siwa, Heracles, with Melqart named once for the god of Tyre, and Achilles.
  Apis, the sacred bull of Memphis (Arrian 3.1); Zeus the King, to whom Alexander sacrifices at Memphis (Arrian 3.5;
  Bosworth, who gives the Greek Basileus).
- English names carry no diacritics except Thaïs. Today's names in the "Today" column keep the diacritics of their
  country's maps: Biga Çayı, Gülek Pass, Türkiye.
- Terms: sarissa (not sarisa), phalanx, Companions or Companion cavalry, hypaspists, satrap and satrapy, hegemon,
  proskynesis, Hellenistic (for the world after Alexander), koine.
