By the 1950s scholars can read the numbers, the calendar and the astronomical tables in Maya writing, but little else. The leading Mayanist, J. Eric S. Thompson, holds that the signs stand for ideas rather than sounds and that the inscriptions record time and religion rather than history. In 1952 the 30-year-old Soviet scholar Yuri Knorozov argues that the "alphabet" Diego de Landa recorded around 1566 is a list of syllables, and that the script joins word signs with syllabic signs. Thompson leads the rejection of his work.

In 1958 Heinrich Berlin identifies the emblem glyphs, royal titles built on a place name. In 1960 Tatiana Proskouriakoff, who had drawn Piedras Negras in 1936 – 1937, shows that the dates on its stelae mark the births, accessions and deaths of rulers, and traces seven kings over two hundred years. The inscriptions record dynastic history.

In December 1973, at the first Mesa Redonda de Palenque, Linda Schele, Floyd Lounsbury and Peter Mathews reconstruct most of the king list of Palenque in one afternoon. More than 90 percent of Maya texts can now be read. Knorozov first visits Guatemala in 1990 and sees Tikal, and Mérida raises a monument to him in 2018. In the same year an airborne laser survey of 2,100 km² of the Maya Biosphere Reserve maps more than 60,000 structures, among them houses, causeways and fortifications. Archaeologists estimate that 7 to 11 million people lived in northern Guatemala between AD 650 and 800.
