In the winter of 1348–1349 the confessions taken at Chillon reach the towns of the Upper Rhine, and one town after another turns on its Jews before the plague arrives. At [Basel](@burning-basel) the guildsmen force their way into the town hall, and the council gives way to them. On 16 January 1349 the Jews are shut into a wooden house on an island in the Rhine and burned. At [Speyer](@killing-speyer) the Jews are killed on 22 January, and their bodies are put into wine casks and thrown into the Rhine. At [Freiburg](@burning-freiburg) the Jews are burned on 30 January.

At [Strasbourg](@burning-strasbourg) the council and its head, the Ammeister Peter Schwarber, protect the Jews. On Monday 9 February the craftsmen gather before the cathedral and force the masters out of office. Schwarber is banished, and a new council is formed. On 14 February the Jews are led to their cemetery and burned in a wooden house built there. Jacob Twinger von Königshofen, who draws on the account of the Strasbourg priest Fritsche Closener, counts about 2,000 dead. Those who accept baptism are spared, and so are the children. The debts owed to the Jews are cancelled, and the council divides their money among the craftsmen. Königshofen writes: “The money was indeed the thing that killed the Jews.” The plague reaches Strasbourg on 8 July.

At [Worms](@burning-worms) on 1 March more than 400 Jews die, many of them in their own houses, which they set on fire rather than accept baptism. At [Erfurt](@burning-erfurt) the community is destroyed on 21 and 22 March. The accounts give from 100 to 3,000 dead, and the plague comes to Erfurt only in 1350. Before the killings a Jewish family hides its silver and gold under the wall of a cellar entrance. Archaeologists find it in 1998: 3,141 silver coins and 14 silver ingots, about 24 kg in all, and more than 700 pieces of goldsmiths’ work. Among them is a gold wedding ring engraved with the Hebrew words mazal tov, “good luck”. Its owner was probably Kalman of Wiehe, a Jewish moneychanger of the town.
