In February 1347 John VI Kantakouzenos enters [Constantinople](@constantinople) and ends six years of civil war. He rules as senior emperor beside the young John V Palaiologos over a small and impoverished empire. The Genoese of Galata, across the Golden Horn, control much of the city’s trade.

By the summer of 1347 ships from [Caffa](@caffa) and the other Black Sea ports have brought the plague to the city. The ships’ course on the map is approximate. From here, by one account, ships carry it on to the Aegean islands, to Egypt and to Italy.

Kantakouzenos later describes the plague in the history of his reign, which he writes after he gives up the throne in 1354 and becomes a monk. For nine passages he borrows almost word for word from the Greek historian Thucydides, who described the plague of Athens in 430 BC. Kantakouzenos writes that the disease came out of northern Scythia and spread along the coasts of the Black Sea to Thrace, Macedonia, Greece, Italy, the islands, Egypt and Syria, “and almost the entire universe”. He describes the swellings, the black blisters, and the sick who cough blood. Some die within the hour, others after two or three days. Strong and weak die alike, he writes, and neither a careful life nor a sound body protects anyone.

Among the dead is the emperor’s youngest son, Andronikos, aged about 13. The scholar Nikephoros Gregoras, who lives in the city, also describes the plague in his history of these years. In a letter to Demetrios Kydones he writes of a city in panic, of medicine that helps nobody and of the dead growing in number.
