In September 1343 a brawl at [Tana](@tana) ends with an official of the Horde dead, killed by the Venetian noble Andreolo Civran. Jani Beg, khan of the Golden Horde, expels the Italians from the town, and they flee by sea to Caffa, the Genoese port in the Crimea. Genoa and Venice stop their ships from trading with the Horde’s ports. The khan’s army follows the Italians to Caffa, and in February 1344 a relief force from Italy drives it from the walls. It returns in 1345, and in 1346 the plague breaks out in its camp.

The best known account of the siege comes from Gabriele de’ Mussi, a notary of Piacenza, writing about 1348. In his account the disease kills the Tatars by the thousand every day. The dying “lost interest in the siege”, he writes, but “ordered corpses to be placed in catapults and lobbed into the city in the hope that the intolerable stench would kill everyone inside”. The defenders throw the bodies into the sea, but the plague breaks out in the city, and ships from Caffa carry it to Italy.

Historians doubt that the corpses brought the plague into Caffa. De’ Mussi stays at Piacenza and writes from what others tell him. Plague passes to people through fleas, and the fleas, it is said, leave a body as it grows cold. The Mongols also hold several other ports of the Crimea, so Caffa is unlikely to be the only port that sends plague ships west.

Ibn al-Wardi, an Arab scholar of Aleppo, cites a judge in the Crimea who put the dead there at 85,000. The plague and the Italian blockade bring Jani Beg to make peace with Genoa and Venice, and in 1347 the Italians return to Tana.
