After the battle Alexander leaves Craterus to build the two new cities, [Nicaea](@nicaea-326b) and [Bucephala](@bucephala-326b), and in summer 326 BC marches east into the monsoon. He crosses the [Acesines](@acesines-326), today the Chenab, in full flood, and then the [Hydraotes](@hydraotes-326), today the Ravi. Beyond it Alexander conquers more land, much of which goes to Porus, now his ally. He reaches the [Hyphasis](@hyphasis-326), today the Beas, meaning to cross and attack the peoples beyond, who were said to have more elephants than any other Indians.

Here the army will go no further. Diodorus says the rain had fallen for seventy days, arms and armour were wearing out, and the men had to dress in Indian cloth. Ahead lay another river, and beyond it armies said to field thousands of elephants. The soldiers meet in camp, and some refuse outright to go further.

Alexander calls his officers together and urges them on. After a long silence Coenus, a senior phalanx commander, speaks for the men: few are left of those who set out, and they long for home. The next day Alexander, angry, says he will go on with volunteers alone, and shuts himself in his tent for three days. He sacrifices for the crossing, and the omens are unfavourable, as Ptolemy tells it. Alexander announces the return, and the soldiers weep for joy.

Before leaving he has twelve altars built, as high as great towers, to thank the gods for his victories; where they stood is not known. He gives Porus all the land up to the Hyphasis. Then he marches back by the way he came, over the Hydraotes and the Acesines, to the Hydaspes, where the fleet is being built.

The march reaches its furthest point east and turns back, and Porus's kingdom grows to the Hyphasis.
