In April 1348 a ship from Sicily, which has had the plague since October 1347, brings it to [Tunis](@tunis). The city has had a new master since September 1347, when Abu al-Hasan Ali, the Marinid sultan of [Fez](@fez), took it from the Hafsid dynasty. He now rules Morocco, [Tlemcen](@tlemcen) and Ifriqiya, the land around Tunis. A great many scholars of the Maghreb, whom he made to follow him, have come to Tunis with his court.

The sultan is also at war. On 10 April 1348 nomad Arab tribes beat his army near Kairouan and besiege him inside the town. His son Abu Inan, governor of Tlemcen, is persuaded there that his father has died, and in June he has himself proclaimed sultan. Only in the summer does Abu al-Hasan get out of Kairouan and sail to Tunis, where the plague is spreading. He dies in 1351 without winning back his throne.

Ibn Khaldun, born in Tunis in May 1332, is about to turn 16. The plague kills his father and his mother and many of the scholars who taught him. Between 1375 and 1378 he writes the Muqaddima, the introduction to his history of the world. In it he looks back on a plague that fell on East and West alike, broke the strength of the states of the day, and left towns empty and buildings in ruins.

From Tunis the plague moves west. One account has it travel the coast and the caravan roads to [Béjaïa](@bejaia), then called Bougie, and to Tlemcen. It breaks out in Fez, the Marinid capital, when the sultan’s soldiers come home from Tunis.
