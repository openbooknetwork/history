In 1349 bands of penitents come out of Austria and Hungary and move west into the Empire. They walk from town to town behind crosses and banners. Twice a day they strip to the waist in a square or a churchyard and beat themselves with whips tipped with iron. A man who joins swears to obey the master of his band for 33 and a half days, one day for each year of Christ’s life. From [Vienna](@vienna) their processions spread through Bavaria and Franconia to the Rhine. Fritsche Closener describes their rites at [Strasbourg](@strasbourg) that summer. In August they reach Flanders, and Gilles Li Muisis, abbot of St Martin’s at [Tournai](@tournai), writes that on the feast of the Assumption, 15 August, “there came from the town of Bruges about 200 men.”

The killings of Jews go on through the year. In late June 1349 Charles IV, king of the Romans, pledges the Jews of [Frankfurt](@burning-frankfurt) to the town and promises that it will go unpunished if the Jews there are killed. In July the Jews are killed and their houses burned. At [Mainz](@burning-mainz), on 24 August, the Jews hold off their attackers at first, but the townsmen overwhelm the Jewish quarter and kill them. At [Cologne](@burning-cologne) the Jews burn themselves in their houses in the night of 23 to 24 August. Charles IV allows [Nuremberg](@burning-nuremberg) to build its new market square on the site of the Jewish quarter. On 5 December at least 562 Jews of Nuremberg are killed, and the Frauenkirche that Charles founds rises over the ruins of their synagogue.

Historians long blamed the flagellants for these killings. Recent research, as the Jewish Museum Frankfurt sums it up, finds that in most towns the bands came after the killings, which the councils had planned.

On 20 October 1349 Clement VI condemns the flagellants in a bull and orders the bishops to suppress them, and the movement fades within months. By 1351 most Jewish communities of the Empire have been destroyed, and many of the survivors move east to Poland, where Casimir III gives them refuge.
