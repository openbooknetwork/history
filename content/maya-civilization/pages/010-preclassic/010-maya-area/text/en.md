The Maya area takes in southeastern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and the western parts of Honduras and El Salvador, about a third of Mesoamerica. In the west it ends near the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, in the southeast along a line from the Lempa River to the Ulúa.

The area has three parts. The highlands are a band of volcanoes, ranges and valleys running east from Chiapas through Guatemala, and Tajumulco, at 4,220 m, is the highest peak. A narrow, fertile plain runs along the Pacific coast. North of the highlands lie the lowlands, a limestone plain that covers the whole Yucatán Peninsula. The southern lowlands are forest, drained by the Usumacinta and its tributaries and by the lower Motagua. The northern lowlands are drier and covered in scrub, and much of their water comes from cenotes, sinkholes in the limestone.

The Mayan languages go back to one language, Proto-Mayan, probably spoken in the Cuchumatanes highlands of Guatemala. By the classification of Lyle Campbell and Terrence Kaufman, the first split comes around 2200 BC, when Huastecan speakers move northwest along the Gulf coast. About 30 Mayan languages are spoken today.

Farmers are at work in the lowlands by about 3000 BC. Around 1800 BC villages on the Pacific coast of Soconusco grow maize, beans, squash and chili peppers. At Cuello in northern Belize, a village from about 1200 BC, maize makes up less than a third of the diet. Deer supply over half the meat, followed by freshwater turtles and dogs.
