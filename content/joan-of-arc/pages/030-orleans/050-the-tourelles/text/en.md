At the south end of the bridge of Orléans, on a pier, stand [the Tourelles](@tourelles), a gatehouse joined by a drawbridge to [the boulevard of the Tourelles](@tourelles-boulevard), an earthwork on the shore. On the city side three arches of the bridge are broken. The English captain William Glasdale holds the fort.

On Saturday 7 May, with the townsmen of Orléans behind Joan, the French attack the boulevard from the south bank.\* From the city the townsmen lay gutters and ladders across the broken arches, a carpenter lengthening the longest gutter, and attack the Tourelles from the bridge too. Ramsay has their guns firing from [the Île Saint-Antoine](@ile-saint-antoine), an island in the Loire. The fight lasts until sunset in the Journal of the Siege, a chronicle of the city, and until eight in the evening by the Bastard of Orléans (later Count of Dunois).

Joan is hit while planting a scaling ladder at the boulevard: an arrow strikes her in the neck, she told her judges in 1431. The Journal and the Bastard put it between the neck and the shoulder, the Chronique de la Pucelle through the shoulder.\*\* She is carried back, the wound is dressed, and she returns. Towards evening, when the captains mean to draw off, she rides apart into a vineyard and prays for about half an hour, as the Bastard remembered. Then she sets her standard at the edge of the ditch, and the French go up the boulevard and take it.

As the English of the boulevard make for the Tourelles, the drawbridge gives way under them.\*\*\* Glasdale, Sir William Moleyns, Sir Hugh Poynings and many others fall into the Loire and drown, and the Tourelles are taken.\*\*\*\* With Saint-Privé empty since the night before, the English hold nothing south of the river. Joan rides back into the city over the patched bridge while the bells ring, by the Chronique, the Bastard and Lowell.\*\*\*\*\*

### Notes

\* The Chronique has her cross with the townsmen's consent and against the will of all the king's captains, and Lowell has the council of war give way to her and the townsmen. Lang differs from Lowell on the captains' resistance.

\*\* Her confessor Jean Pasquerel puts the wound above the breast. The hour is given as the morning (the Chronique), after dinner (Pasquerel), early in the afternoon (Lowell) and after the boulevard was won (Ramsay).

\*\*\* The Journal says only that it gave way under them, and the Chronique that it broke by the judgement of God. Quicherat cites the town's accounts of 1429, which pay for a boat burned under the bridge of the Tourelles, and Lowell has the fire spread from that boat to the drawbridge. The chronicle of the feast of 8 May tells of the fire-boat and says the English had cut the bridge themselves to trap the French.

\*\*\*\* Charles VII's letter of 10 May puts the English there at about 600 under two banners and the standard of Chandos, all killed or taken. The chronicle of the feast has 500 to 600 picked men, the Journal 400 to 500, all killed or drowned but a few prisoners, and the Chronique 500, of whom about 200 were taken alive. Ramsay gives 300 prisoners.

\*\*\*\*\* The Journal has her stay the night in the field with the captains, guarding the Tourelles and watching for the English of Saint-Laurent.
