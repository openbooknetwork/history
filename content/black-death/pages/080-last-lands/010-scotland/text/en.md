From June to August 1349 the plague is at its worst in the north of England, and the Scots take it for God’s punishment of their old enemy. Their king, David II, has been a prisoner in England since his defeat at Neville’s Cross in 1346. Henry Knighton, a canon of Leicester, writes that the Scots swore “by the foul death of England” and gathered an army in the forest around [Selkirk](@selkirk) in the autumn of 1349 to invade the stricken kingdom.

The plague strikes the camp before the army can march. By Knighton’s account about 5,000 men die within a short time, and the rest, some weak and some strong, set out for home. The English follow them and kill many on the way. The men who reach home carry the plague with them, it is said, and in 1350 it spreads through the Lowlands and, by one account, north across the Forth. No source names its roads, so the lines on the map are approximate.

John of Fordun, a Scottish priest who compiled his annals around 1363, puts the great plague in Scotland in 1350. He writes that the flesh of the sick swelled and that they lived barely two days. In his account it struck the common people more than the lords, and it killed nearly a third of mankind.

The Scottish evidence for the plague comes down to a few sentences in the chronicles, so historians have little to test Fordun’s figure against. For England they can check the chroniclers against manor court rolls and bishops’ registers.
